# 1. BRIEF SUMMARY

Dipeptidyl Peptidase 4 (DPP-4, EC 3.4.14.5) is a membrane bound protease and expressed in many tissues including kidneys, liver, intestine, lymphocytes and vascular endothelial cells. A significant level of DPP-4 activity is also observed in plasma, which likely originates from multiple tissues that express the enzyme. The most important physiological substrates of DPP-4 are the incretins Glucagon-Like Peptide-1 (GLP-1) and Glucose-dependent Insulinotropic Peptide (GIP). DPP-4 inhibition was shown to enhance levels of these endogenous incretin hormones. Both GLP-1 and GIP exert potent glucose-dependent insulinotropic actions and thereby contribute to the maintenance of post-meal glycemic control.

Linagliptin is a potent inhibitor (IC₅₀ = 1 nM) of human Dipeptidyl Peptidase 4 (DPP-4) and exhibits excellent selectivity versus a variety of proteases including DPP-8 and DPP-9 (> 10,000-fold). In healthy as well as obese and diabetic animals (Zucker *fa/fa* rat, Zucker Diabetic Fatty Rat (ZDF) and *db/db* mice) linagliptin enhanced glucose-induced elevations of intact GLP-1 and insulin and lowered glucose levels with an ED₅₀ of 1 mg/kg and below.  These data indicate that linagliptin is an efficacious anti-diabetic drug. The main metabolite of linagliptin CD 1790 neither inhibited DPP-4 activity nor interacted with a variety of receptors, channels and enzymes.

Metformin belongs structurally to the biguanides and is an insulin sensitizing drug. The antidiabetic action of metformin is based on decreased hepatic glucose production via suppressed gluconeogenesis, decreased absorption of glucose from the intestine and improved insulin sensitivity. The pharmacological action of metformin has minor or no effect on insulin secretion, but reduces the demand of insulin due to its insulin sensitizing effect by increasing peripheral uptake of glucose.

The combination of linagliptin with metformin resulted in an additive reduction of glucose excursion in diabetic *db/db* mice.

Linagliptin has a pharmacological profile that suggests good tolerability. Safety pharmacology studies did not indicate a risk of arrhythmia including those associated with a prolongation of the QT interval. No relevant effects on the ECG were observed in safety pharmacology and toxicology studies in the Cynomolgus monkey at dosages up to and including 300 mg/kg/day (2523-fold clinical cmax). In addition, the safety pharmacology assessment of neurological (CNS) and respiratory effects did not identify any significant liabilities of linagliptin. No safety pharmacology studies with metformin and the linagliptin/metformin combination have been performed. L`<text:span text:style-name="Highlight">linagliptin is currently under CHMP review. Additional information regarding linagliptin in comparison to the linagliptin mono submission is highlighted in yellow.</text:span>`{=opendocument}

In conclusion, the administration of linagliptin and metformin did result in an additive effect on glucose reduction in diabetic animals. The general pharmacology and safety pharmacology profile of linagliptin does not favour an aggravation of the well-known side effects of metformin.



# 2. PRIMARY PHARMACODYNAMIC STUDIES



## 2.1 EXPRESSION AND FUNCTION OF DIPEPTIDYL PEPTIDASE 4 (DPP-4)



Dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP-4, EC 3.4.14.5) is a membrane bound protease which specifically cleaves dipeptides after a penultimate N-terminal proline or alanine. DPP-4 is widely expressed in many tissues including kidneys, liver, intestine, lymphocytes and vascular endothelial cells. A significant level of DPP-4 activity is also observed in plasma, which likely originates from multiple tissues that express the enzyme. DPP-4 plays a key role in the degradation of a number of peptide hormones, neuropeptides and chemokines, thus regulating their half-life and biological activity. The most important physiological substrates of DPP-4 are glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and glucose-dependent insulinotropic peptide (GIP) [R04-2662, R10-0834]. The incretin GLP-1 is produced in intestinal endocrine L-cells and GIP in intestinal K cells.

Both GLP-1 and GIP exert potent glucose-dependent insulinotropic actions and thereby contribute to the maintenance of post-prandial glycemic control [R10-0829, R04-2666, R04-2661, R10-0833, R04-2665].

Consistent with a significant role for GLP-1 and GIP in glucose homeostasis, receptor knockout for either peptide, produces glucose intolerance in mice [R10-0830, R10-0831]. Additionally, preclinical and clinical studies, in acute or subchronic regimens, have demonstrated the efficacy of GLP-1, GIP or their peptide analogues in improving glucose homeostasis.

## 2.2 MECHANISTIC ACTION OF METFORMIN



Metformin belongs structurally to the biguanides and is an insulin sensitizing drug which improves fasted and postprandial plasma glucose in patients with type 2 diabetes. The mechanism of action of metformin is still not elucidated in detail, however its anti-diabetic action is based on decreased hepatic glucose production via suppressed gluconeogenesis, decreased absorption of glucose from the intestine and improved insulin sensitivity [R10-5440].

The pharmacological action of metformin has minor or no effect on insulin secretion, but reduces the demand of insulin due to its insulin sensitizing effect by increasing peripheral uptake of glucose. The pharmacological actions of metformin are, therefore, targeted towards the liver, the muscle and the adipose tissue. Further, metformin is not associated with increased risk of hypoglycaemic episodes. The combination of an insulin sensitizing agent with a glucose-dependent insulin secretagogue combines an ideal and also safe principle for diabetes therapy in patients with type 2 diabetes. Moreover, metformin was shown to increase GLP-1 release in obese patients with or without type 2 diabetes [R10-5241], including its active (non-cleaved) form [R10-5491]. The mechanism behind the elevation of GLP-1 is independent of DPP-4. This observation provides further evidence of the significance of the combination for both therapeutic approaches.



## 2.3 IN VITRO SELECTIVITY OF LINAGLIPTIN AND ITS METABOLITES [U06-1338, MODULE 4.2.1.1], [U05-2007, MODULE 4.2.1.1], [U05-1968, MODULE 4.2.1.1], [U06-1453, MODULE 4.2.1.1], [U06-1133, MODULE 4.2.1.1], [U09-1045, MODULE 4.2.1.1], [U09-2411, MODULE 4.2.1.1]



Linagliptin is a tight binding and competitive, reversible inhibitor of human DPP-4. X-ray co-crystallography demonstrated that it binds specifically to the active site of the enzyme [P08-01945]. Linagliptin exhibited an IC₅₀ of 1 nM in extracts of human-derived CaCo-2 cells that express high levels of DPP-4. Further, DPP-4 activity of human plasma is inhibited by linagliptin with an IC₅₀ of 3.6 nM [U05-1995, Module 4.2.1.1]. DPP-4 activity in plasma of mice, rats and dogs was inhibited to a similar degree indicated by IC₅₀ values of 10.2 nM, 6.8 nM and 10.4 nM, respectively. Also DPP-4 from plasma of Cynomolgus monkey was inhibited potently [U11-1453, Module 4.2.1.1] by linagliptin. In vivo, the DPP-4 activity in Rhesus monkeys and Beagle dogs was inhibited >70% following dosing with 1 mg/kg within 30 min and was maintained maximally for 7 h.

Linagliptin was also tested in a series of 50 different molecular targets for receptor binding and on 27 *in vitro* enzyme assays systems. At a concentration of 10 µM, linagliptin did not show an inhibition above 31% percent in each of the enzymatic assays tested. None of the 50 different targets for receptor binding have been affected to more than 50% in a concentration of 3 µM with the exception of the human muscarinic receptors M1 (83%), M2 (82%) and M3 (55%) [U05-2007, Module 4.2.1.1]. Further detailed IC₅₀ determination in binding assays revealed IC50 of 295 nM, 1000 nM and 840 nM for M1, M2 and M3, respectively [U05-1968, Module 4.2.1.1]. Therefore, the potential of linagliptin to show off-target inhibitory activity at clinically relevant doses is considered minimal.

Linagliptin showed a similar maximal efficacy for inhibition of DPP-4 *in vitro* when compared with other DPP-4 inhibitors, but with a higher potency (IC₅₀~1 nM for linagliptin vs. 19, 62, 50 and 24 nM, for sitagliptin, vildagliptin, saxagliptin and alogliptin, respectively) [P08-06359].

For linagliptin the main metabolite CD 1790 (pure S-enantiomer) was identified, which was also tested in its racemic form (CD 1750). CD 1750 neither inhibited DPP-4 activity at a concentration of 1µM nor interacted CD 1750 with plasmin, trypsin, thrombin, DPP-2 and FAP at this concentration [U06-1453, Module 4.2.1.1]. CD 1790 also did not inhibit DPP-4 activity at a concentration of 1 µM. [U09-2411, Module 4.2.1.1]. CD 1790 was further tested for activity on 54 molecular targets for receptor binding and on 21 enzymatic assays that were used also for the linagliptin receptor screen. CD 1750 [U06-1133, Module 4.2.1.1] or CD 1790 [U09-1045, Module 4.2.1.1] had no effect above 33% inhibition on either molecular receptors or enzyme assays in the concentration of 3 µM and 10 µM, respectively.  Exceptions were the enzymatic stimulation of human cyclooxygenase-1 and -2 activity (51%). However, stimulation of enzymatic activity depends on assay conditions and variations rather than on relevant compound enzyme interactions. Therefore, this finding is considered to be without biological significance.

## 2.5 PHARMACODYNAMIC EFFECTS OF LINAGLIPTIN IN VIVO [U05-1995, MODULE 4.2.1.1]



### 2.5.1 Studies in Rats, Beagle Dogs and Rhesus Monkeys



These investigations were performed to study the effect of linagliptin on plasma DPP-4 activity in rat, dog and monkey. In addition, an oral Glucose Tolerance Test (oGTT) was performed in rats to demonstrate the potency of linagliptin in improving glycemic control.



:::{custom-paragraph="Figure footer"}

Figure 2.5.1: 1 Dose dependent effect of linagliptin on plasma DPP-4 activity in Wistar rats after oral administration [U05-1995]

:::



Linagliptin orally administered to non-fasted male Wistar rats at a dosage of 1 mg/kg produced a rapid and dose-dependent inhibition of plasma DPP-4 activity (Figure 2.5.1: 1). The compound inhibited post-dose plasma DPP-4 activity by 70% at 7 h and by 51% at 24 h [U05-1995, Module 4.2.1.1]. The ED₅₀ of linagliptin in inhibiting DPP-4 was determined to be 0.26 mg/kg 7 h post-dose and 0.69 mg/kg 24 h post-dose. These data indicate a high *in vivo* potency of linagliptin and are suggestive of a long duration of action in Wistar rats.

In order to assess the effect of linagliptin on plasma DPP-4 activity in other species, a single oral dose of the compound was administered at a dosage of 1 mg/kg to Beagle dogs and Rhesus monkeys. Linagliptin produced a significant inhibition (>70%) of plasma DPP-4 in both species within 0.5 h post dosing. In Beagle dogs, the DPP-4 inhibitory effect was maintained up to 7 h, and disappeared subsequently over time such that by 48 h the plasma DPP-4 activity was similar to pre-dose levels. In Rhesus monkeys, the effect appeared to last longer such that at 72 h there was still approximately 20% inhibition of plasma DPP-4 [U05-1995].





### 2.5.2 Studies in C57BL/6J Mice



The effect of an acute oral administration of linagliptin on glucose control during an oGTT was evaluated in C57BL/6J mice. In vehicle treated C57BL/6J mice, peak glucose levels (18.6 ± 1.5 mM) occurred at 30 min after glucose load. This increase in plasma glucose excursion was significantly reduced by doses of 1 and 3 mg/kg linagliptin (to 13.0 ± 1.0 mM, p<0.05 and 13.0 ± 0.6 mM, p<0.001, respectively). The AUC for glucose excursion was also suppressed significantly by linagliptin (-49% for 1 mg/kg dose and -55% for the 3 mg/kg dose; both p<0.001) (Figure 2.5.2: 1).



:::{custom-paragraph="Figure footer"}

Figure 2.5.2: 1 Effect of oral administration of linagliptin on glucose AUC in C57BL/6J mice. Indicated p-values above bars are calculated versus control (\*\*\*, p< 0.001)

:::



Plasma DPP-4 activity, measured in the same study at the 180 min time-point, was inhibited by 71% at 1 mg/kg dose and by 75% at the 3 mg/kg dose (both p<0.0001) [U05-1995, Module 4.2.1.1]. In conclusion, linagliptin is a highly potent compound having an efficacious dose of 1 mg/kg or less in C57BL/6J mice.

In order to evaluate the *duration* of action of linagliptin, the compound was tested in a 2-day study, using also male C57BL/6J mice. A single dose of linagliptin was given at a dosage of 0.3, 1 or 3 mg/kg 16 h before an oGTT was performed [U05-1995].



:::{custom-paragraph="Figure footer"}

Figure 2.5.2: 2 oGTT in C57BL/6J mice 16 h after dosing with linagliptin (p.o.) (A) Plasma DPP-4 activity (B) Plasma glucose

:::



In vehicle-treated mice, peak glucose levels (20.2 ± 1.6 mM) occurred 30 min after glucose load and returned subsequently towards baseline at 180 min post glucose. The increase in plasma glucose excursion was significantly reduced by all doses of linagliptin and in parallel the DPP-4 activity was reduced in a similar way (Figure 2.5.2: 2).

These data demonstrate that orally administered linagliptin had a long duration of action in C57BL/6J mice in terms of both inhibition of DPP-4 and glycemic control.



### 2.5.3 Studies in C57BL/KsJdb/db (*db/db*) Mice



The C57BL/KsJdb/db (*db/db*) mouse is a model of insulin resistance and glucose intolerance, which with age develops overt diabetes. In this model, oral dosing of linagliptin 45 minutes before initiation of an oGTT produced a dose-dependent improvement in the oGTT profile.

AUC (180 min) for glucose excursion was suppressed by 62% at a dose of 1 mg/kg linagliptin (p<0.001) (Figure 2.5.3: 1) [U05-1995, Module 4.2.1.1].

### 2.5.4 Studies in Zucker Fatty (*fa/fa*) Rats



In the obese Zucker Fatty (*fa/fa*) rat, a model of glucose intolerance, oral dosing of linagliptin 17 h prior to an oGTT inhibited plasma DPP-4 activity by >70% within 20 min and this was maintained for the duration of the oGTT (180 min) (Figure 2.5.4: 1 A). Peak glucose levels (20 minutes after glucose load) were significantly reduced by linagliptin (vehicle: 12.2 +0.77 mM, 1 mg/kg linagliptin: 8.9 +0.4 mM, p<0.01), (Figure 2.5.4: 1 B). The AUC180 min for glucose excursion was also significantly suppressed by linagliptin (-38.5%, p<0.05) [U05-1995].

At this time point, vehicle-treated and linagliptin-treated groups were dosed orally with glucose. Compared to vehicle, the glucose induced elevations of intact GLP-1 and of insulin were more pronounced in animals that had received linagliptin (Figure 2.5.4: 2) [U05-1995, Module 4.2.1.1].

### 2.5.5 Studies in Zucker Diabetic Fatty (ZDF) Rats

Application of linagliptin (1 mg/kg and 3 mg/kg) to ZDF rats inhibited DPP-4 plasma activity by >80% within 20 minutes and was maintained for the duration of the oGTT (180 min) (Figure 2.5.5: 1 A and B).

### 2.5.6 Subchronic Application of Linagliptin and Effect ond Euclycemic Hyperinsulinemic Clamp Studies in db/ db Mice [U10-1426, Module 4.2.1.1]



The aim of this study was to demonstrate the efficacy of linagliptin on HbA1C during long term treatment of diabetic *db/db* mice. In addition it was assessed if linagliptin has an influence on the insulin sensitivity in these animals. A dose of 3 mg/kg/day was administered daily for 8 weeks to female *db/db* mice (8 weeks old when treatment started, n=15 per group).  During the experiment food intake, water uptake and glucose parameters were monitored. At the end of the experiment an euglycemic-hyperinsulinemic clamp was performed to assess changes in the insulin sensitivity and glucose tissue distribution. Linagliptin was well tolerated. After 8 weeks of linagliptin treatment fed plasma glucose was significantly reduced by 3 mg/kg/day linagliptin (starting from day 21 on). In the placebo group a significant deterioration of HbA1c values during the entire course of the study was observed.  In contrast, in the linagliptin treatment group, HbA1c values increased compared to baseline until treatment day 28 and did not further increase up to 56 days of treatment. Significantly (up to 1%; \*, p<0.05) lower HbA1c values were found in animals treated with linagliptin (day 35 to 56) compared to placebo (Figure 2.5.6: 1).

Euglycemic-hyperinsulinemic clamps performed at the end of the experiment revealed a slight tendency for improvement of insulin sensitivity in the linagliptin treatment group compared to placebo. This difference was not statistically significant and is probably due to the severe insulin resistance in *db/db* mice.



:::{custom-paragraph="Figure footer"}

Figure 2.5.6: 1 HbA1c values during the study course

:::



In vehicle treated ZDF rats, peak glucose levels (19.1 ± 1.4 mM) occurred 20 min post glucose load and glucose levels remained significantly elevated during the entire period of oGTT, indicative of severe insulin resistance and defective glucose disposal. At 40 min, glucose levels in vehicle treated rats were only slightly reduced to 18.5 ± 1.5 mM. At this time point linagliptin caused a significant reduction in glucose levels to 15.0 ± 0.8 mM (1 mg/kg, p<0.05) and 13.4 ± 1.3 mM (3 mg/kg, p<0.03) (Figure 2.5.5: 1 B).

In conclusion, the *in vivo* data obtained from different species and disease models during acute treatment demonstrate a potent anti-diabetic action of linagliptin.

## 2.7 PHARMACODYNAMIC EFFECTS OF THE LINAGLIPTIN/ METFORMIN COMBINATION IN VIVO [U09-1799, MODULE 4.2.1.1]

Male *db/db* mice were orally dosed once daily for one week with either vehicle, linagliptin (1 mg/kg), metformin (200 mg/kg), or a combination of linagliptin (1 mg/kg) and metformin (200 mg/kg). An oGTT was performed 16 h after the last compound administration (Figure 2.7: 1). The combined treatment of linagliptin and metformin resulted in a significant reduction of the AUC for glucose excursion by 37%.

# 3. SECONDARY PHARMACODYNAMIC STUDIES WITH LINAGLITPIN



Secondary pharmacodynamic studies (non-GLP) were performed to investigate neurological, cardiovascular, pulmonary, gastrointestinal and renal effects. Single doses were given.

## 3.1 EFFECTS ON THE CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM [U04-1103, MODULE 4.2.1.2]



Possible effects of linagliptin on CNS functions were investigated in mice. This involved testing effects on general behavior (using a modified Irwin test) over 24 h and on nocturnal locomotor activity over 14 h. Linagliptin was administered orally by gavage to 7 mice per group at dose levels of 0, 3, 10 or 30 mg/kg. General behavior was measured through series of observations and tests and in addition, body temperature was measured. The locomotor activity of each mouse was recorded in test chambers with 5 horizontal infrared light barriers.

Linagliptin at dosages up to 30 mg/kg had no effects on general behavior as measured in a modified Irwin test or nocturnal motility in mice.

## 3.2 EFFECTS ON THE CARDIOVASCULAR AND RESPIRATORY SYSTEM [U04-1093, MODULE 4.2.1.2]



Possible effects of linagliptin on the cardiovascular or respiratory systems of the conscious rat were investigated following oral administration of the drug [U04-1093]. Respiratory parameters were measured using a whole body plethysmograph system. Linagliptin was administered oral by gavage to 8 rats per group at doses of 0, 3, 10 or 30 mg/kg. Measurements were recorded continuously for 7 h post drug application.

There was no relevant effect on any measured parameters.

## 3.3 EFFECTS ON HEMODYNAMICS AND ECG IN CONSCIOUS DOGS [U04-1566, MODULE 4.2.1.2]



Studies were conducted in conscious Labrador dogs that had been instrumented for the telemetric collection of arterial blood pressure, left ventricular pressure, ECG and temperature data. Dogs were treated with oral doses of 0, 1, 3 or 10 mg/kg of linagliptin and hemodynamic data were collected over 7 h.

No effects on any of the parameters measured were seen.



## 3.4 EFFECTS ON RENAL FUNCTION AND CLINICAL CHEMISTRY [U04-1053, MODULE 4.2.1.2]



Studies were conducted in conscious rats treated orally with 3, 10 or 30 mg/kg linagliptin for assessing possible effects on renal function and clinical chemistry parameters reflecting changes in renal function, liver function or other metabolic effects [U04-1053].

There was no effect on the urine volume or the parameters measured in urine (urine volume, osmolality, pH, [Na+], [K+], [Cl-], [Ca2+], [Mg2+], creatinine, glucose, protein, AST, ALT, GGT, LDH, ALP, and ß-NAG). In serum, the majority of measured parameters were unaffected, including osmolarity, creatinine, glucose, BUN, triglycerides, conjugated bilirubin, AST, GGT, GLDH, LDH, ALP, creatine kinase and the electrolytes [Na+], [K+], [Cl-], [Ca2+] and [Mg2+]. The highest dose (30 mg/kg) led to an increase in serum protein at 8 h post-administration and fat metabolism (increased protein, cholesterol and free fatty acids) 24 h post-administration. Serum ALT and total bilirubin were found to be slightly increased.

Based on the endpoints of the study linagliptin did not influence kidney function or integrity.

## 3.5 EFFECTS ON THE GASTROINTESTINAL SYSTEM [U04-1013, MODULE 4.2.1.2, U04-1012, MODULE 4.2.1.2]



Possible effects of linagliptin on the gastrointestinal system were assessed in rats following administration of 3, 10 or 30 mg/kg given either intraduodenally [U04-1012] or orally [U04-1013].

After intraduodenal administration of linagliptin, the effect on gastric secretion was assessed in pylorus-ligated rats. Linagliptin given at doses of 3 to 30 mg/kg had no significant effect on the gastric secretion parameters pH and total acidity. Gastric juice volume was reduced dose-dependently at 10 and 30 mg/kg. Thus, an inhibition of acid output was calculated, reaching significance only at the 10 mg/kg dose. The dose of 3 mg/kg had no effect on gastric secretion parameters. The doses of 10 and 30 mg/kg led to a slight reduction in gastric juice volume and thereby acid output.

Effects on gastrointestinal motility were assessed in rats following oral administration of 3, 10 or 30 mg/kg linagliptin. There was no inhibition on the rate of gastric emptying following the administration of linagliptin. However, there was a slight and significant reduction (about 10%) in the intestinal transit after each dose, without evidence of a dose-dependency.

In conclusion, effects on the gastrointestinal tract were minimal and were considered to reflect an exaggerated pharmacodynamic effect of linagliptin.



No secondary pharmacodynamic studies with **metformin** and the **linagliptin/metformin combination** have been performed at Boehringer Ingelheim.



# 4. SAFETY PHARMACOLOGY STUDIES WITH LINAGLIPTIN



These core battery studies (ICHS7A and ICHS7B) were all conducted according to Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) except for the *in vitro* assays. Single doses were given if not stated otherwise. The calculated exposure multiples to clinical use are based on a C~max,ss~ of 11.1 nM as clinical exposure [see Clinical Summary, Module 2.7.2].

## 4.1 EFFECTS ON THE CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM [U05-1935, MODULE 4.2.1.3]



The purpose of this modified Irwin study was to evaluate possible side-effects on general behavior, body temperature and spontaneous locomotor activity.

Linagliptin was administered as a single oral dose to groups of 4 male Crl:WI(Han) rats at dose levels of 6, 60, or 600 mg/kg. No marked or consistent behavioral or physiological changes were seen. In addition, no significant effects on body temperature or spontaneous locomotory activity were observed.

## 4.2 EFFECTS ON THE CARDIOVASCULAR SYSTEM



### 4.2.1 Electrophysiologic Parameters *in vitro* [U04-1088, Module 4.2.1.3]



This non-GLP study was conducted to determine the influence of linagliptin on electrophysiologic parameters *in vitro*. In particular, the pro-arrhythmic potential due to prolongation of myocardial repolarization was assessed. For this evaluation effects on hERG (human Ether-a-go-go Related Gene)-mediated potassium current and effects on action potential configuration in the isolated Guinea pig papillary muscle were determined.

Experiments on hERG-mediated potassium current were performed using HEK293 (Human Embryonic Kidney) cells stably expressing the hERG-mediated potassium channel.  Whole-cell experiments were carried out by means of the patch-clamp technique. Effects on the hERG-mediated current amplitude by linagliptin were measured for each test concentration (1.0, 3.0, 10 µM) over 5 min. Linagliptin had no effect on hERG-mediated potassium current in concentrations up to 10µM.

Furthermore, the effect of linagliptin on the action potential configuration in Guinea pig papillary muscles was assessed. In Guinea pig papillary muscles exposed to concentrations of linagliptin up to 10 µM, resting membrane potential, action potential amplitude and overshoot, and maximal upstroke velocity were not affected. There was, however, a concentration-dependent shortening of the action potential beginning at 0.3 µM which increased up to a 7% shortening (of APD90) at 10 µM.

These studies suggested that linagliptin has a low pro-arrhythmic potential based on a delayed ventricular repolarization.



### 4.2.2 Cardiovascular Effects *in vivo* (Telemetered Cynomolgus Monkey) [U06-1700, Module 4.2.3.7.7]



The objective of the study was to evaluate potential effects of linagliptin on the cardiovascular system in the conscious telemetered Cynomolgus monkey.

The study investigated any potential effects on blood pressure, heart rate and the ECG (RR, PR, QRS, QT, and corrected QT intervals). One group of 4 animals was used consisting of 2 male and 2 female monkeys, surgically instrumented with telemetry transmitters. Each animal received vehicle (0 mg/kg) and three doses of linagliptin (12, 60 and 150 mg/kg) by oral gavage.

Toxicokinetic data indicated an adequate and dose-related exposure. 1 h after administration plasma concentrations of 18900 nM (1703x clinical C~máx~) were reached at a dosage of 150 mg/kg.

There were no relevant treatment-related changes in the ECG (lead II) at dosages up to 150 mg/kg. At dosages of 60 and 150 mg/kg, mild (up to 16.7%) but statistically significant heart rate increases were reported. Due to its small absolute size and the missing dose relationship, the effect was judged to be not of toxicological relevance.

### 4.2.3 Cardiovascular Effects *in vivo* in Repeat-Dose Toxicity Studies



Cardiovascular investigations were also conducted as part of repeat-dose toxicity studies [see Toxicology Written summary, Module 2.6.6]. In the 4-week toxicity study in Beagle dogs dosages of up to 9 mg/kg/day (210-fold clinical C~máx~) were free from any clinical relevant effect of linagliptin on blood pressure, heart rate and ECG. Changes seen at 45 mg/kg/day (955-fold clinical C~máx~) were considered to be pseudo-allergy related [U05-1944, Module 4.2.3.2]. In the Cynomolgus monkey, in which no pseudo-allergy was observed, no treatment related changes were detected in the electrocardiographic and blood pressure measurements in the 4-week, 3-month and 12-month toxicity studies [U05-2481, Module 4.2.3.2, U07-1072, Module 4.2.3.2, U08-1185, Module 4.2.3.2] at dosages up to 300 mg/kg/day associated with a C~máx~ value of 28000 nM (2523-fold clinical C~máx~).

In conclusion, the preclinical safety data indicated no linagliptin-related cardiovascular risk.



## 4.3 EFFECTS ON RESPIRATORY FUNCTION [U05-1967, MODULE 4.2.1.3]



The objective of this study was to assess possible effects of linagliptin on respiratory parameters in the conscious rat.

Linagliptin was administered as a single oral dose to groups of 8 male rats at dosages of 6, 60 and 600 mg/kg in order to assess effects on respiration rate, tidal volume and minute volume. Two Wistar strains were used due to the fact that an incorrect strain (Wistar Crl:(WI)BR) instead of the requested Crl:WI (Han) strain was utilized first.

Linagliptin at oral dosages of 6 or 60 mg/kg produced no effect on respiratory rate, tidal volume and minute volume in both Wistar strains in comparison to the vehicle-treated control group.

At a dose of 600 mg/kg a statistically significant increase in tidal volume at 30 min post-dose was seen in Wistar Crl:(WI)BR rats. In Crl:WI(Han) rats a dosage of 600 mg/kg produced a significant decrease in respiration rate and minute volume at 30 min post-dose.

Toxicokinetic measurements performed in the 4-week toxicity study in rats with identical dose level showed that a dose of 60 mg/kg was associated with 494x clinical C~máx~, and a dose of 600 mg/kg was associated with 3099x clinical C~máx~ [U05-1937, Module 4.2.3.2].

Therefore, the slight and isolated respiratory effects seen at 600 mg/kg are considered to be without relevance for human safety.



No safety pharmacology studies with **metformin** or the **combination of metformin and linagliptin** have been performed.

# 6. OTHER PHARMACOLOGY STUDIES WITH LINAGLIPTIN



## 6.1 WOUND HEALING STUDY IN HEALTHY MICE [U08-2241, MODULE 4.2.1.3]



The selectivity of linagliptin versus other proteases was demonstrated to be more than 10,000- fold [U06-1338, Module 4.2.1.1] with the exception of a 94-fold selectivity over Fibroblast Activation Protein (FAP). The latter protease plays a so far unknown role in wound healing processes and tissue remodelling [P97-10076]. For that reason the aim of the study was to determine the effect of linagliptin on wound healing in mice [U08-2241].

Eighty male C57Bl6 mice, 10 weeks of age at the start of the experiment, were used. Dosages of 30, 10, 3, 1 and 0.3 mg/kg were administered. All mice except 10 underwent surgical wounding in their dorsal skin, two wounds per mouse. The healing of these wounds and the effect of linagliptin on the wound healing was monitored by photographing the wounds every 2 to 3 days during the experimental period. After 2 weeks all mice were sacrificed and plasma samples were collected.

Oral treatment with linagliptin via gavage had no (negative) effect on the general well being and the body weight of the mice. During the start of the experimental period the surface area of the wounds was 20.48 ± 5.65 mm² (herein defined as 100%). Seven days later the average wound area was 10.41 ±6.61 mm² (50.8% of day 0) and 14 days later the wounds were almost completely healed (wound surface area of 0.09 ±0.24 mm² or 0.4% of day 0). No significant differences in wound area could be seen at any time point between all groups. The DPP-4 activity measured in the plasma of the respective animals was inhibited by 95%, 90%, 87.1%, 81.2% and 78.1% for the doses of 30, 10, 3, 1 and 0.3 mg/kg linagliptin.  `<text:span text:style-name="Highlight">To potentially differentiate between DPP-4 and FAP related effects, in this study also a structural related DPP-4 inhibitor (BI 14361) with similar efficacy on DPP-4 (IC₅₀ =2nM) but with high selectivity against FAP (IC50=2µM) was included. A dose of 30 mg/kg was also without effect on the wound healing process in the respective mice.</text:span>`{=opendocument}

In conclusion, linagliptin at dosages up to 30 mg/kg/day had no negative effect on woundhealing in healthy mice. This indicates that the FAP inhibition measured *in vitro* did not induce a relevant in vivo effect.

No further other pharmacodynamic studies have been performed for linagliptin, metformin and the combination of metformin and linagliptin.



# 7. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS



**Linagliptin** is a potent, selective and competitive reversible inhibitor of DPP-4 *in vitro* with an IC₅₀ of 1 nM. Clinical peak plasma levels of 11.1 nM have been shown to be effective and are reached at the MRHD of 5 mg q.d..

The selectivity of linagliptin versus various proteases was 10,000-fold and more, thereby minimizing the risk of having side effects. The exception regarding selectivity concerns Fibroblast Activation Protein (FAP) where selectivity was tested to be 94-fold (IC₅₀=94 nM).  FAP is one of the closest homologues of DPP-4 and is generally not expressed under healthy conditions. FAP is known to be expressed in regenerating tissue and during tissue remodelling processes such as fibrosis, carcinogenesis and wound healing, however of unknown function [P97-10076]. In all nonclinical studies performed with linagliptin, there was no *in vivo* effect, which could be attributed to a potential FAP-inhibition. Therefore, the FAP inhibition seen *in vitro* is considered to have no in vivo or clinical relevance. A potential FAP inhibition *in vivo* was specifically investigated in a wound healing study in mice, as FAP is known to be expressed in regenerating tissue. In this wound healing study [U08-2241, Module 4.2.1.3], linagliptin was administered at doses up to 30 mg/kg/day ensuring a full potential FAP inhibition over 24 h. No negative effect on wound healing was observed. In addition, in toxicological studies dosage levels associated with plasma concentrations far beyond 94 nM over 24 h - and thereby assuming full inhibition of FAP - did not cause any adverse or potential FAP-related effect (e.g. the NOAEL of the 12-month toxicity study in Cynomolgus monkeys was 10 mg/kg/day, associated with a C~max,ss~ of 2050 nM and an AUC~0-24h,ss~ of 6270 nM⋅h).

Linagliptin is highly active and inhibits dose-dependently DPP-4 activity in several species (rat, mice, dog, monkey). A dose of 1 mg/kg causes a more than 70% inhibition of the enzyme. The effect on DPP-4 activity in plasma is long lasting because of the specific pharmacokinetic features of the drug (long half-life due to tight binding of linagliptin to the target enzyme DPP-4 [see Pharmacokinetics Written Summary, Module 2.6.4]). This translates in a significant inhibition of the enzyme even 16 h post-dose in the respective animals. Linagliptin exhibited potent effects on glucose excursion in healthy animals due to the increase of GLP-1 and insulin secretion. Moreover, in several models representing type 2 diabetes linagliptin exhibited potent reduction of blood glucose levels following glucose tolerance tests with calculated ED₅₀ values below 1 mg/kg in rats and mice. In models reflecting more severe stages of diabetes like *db/db* mice of ZDF rats, however, the magnitude of the effect is lower than that observed in C57BL/6J mice, due to the severe insulin resistance in the ZDF rat and db/db mouse model. However, the clinically relevant long-term glucose parameter HbA1C is significantly reduced by linagliptin in this model.

The efficacy of linagliptin is additive to the anti-diabetic effect of metformin in the subchronic treatment regimen, thus providing a scientific rationale for combination of these two pharmacological principles.

The main metabolite of linagliptin CD 1790 (S-enantiomer) did not interact with receptors or enzymes at a concentration up to 3 µM. In addition, no influence of CD 1790 on DPP-4 activity in a concentration up to 1 µM and on related proteases up to 3 µM were observed. These observations suggest a negligible role of the metabolites of linagliptin.

Linagliptin has a pharmacological profile that suggests good tolerability. Safety pharmacology studies did not indicate a risk of arrhythmia including those associated with a prolongation of the QT interval. No relevant effects on the ECG were observed in safety pharmacology and toxicity studies at dosages up to 300 mg/kg/day.

Lactic acidosis is the most frequent cause of metabolic acidosis and is characterized by a lactate concentration exceeding 5 mM and a pH <7.35 [R10-5439]. Biguanides have well defined effects on glucose/lactate metabolism, which also contribute to their anti-diabetic properties in blocking hepatic gluconeogenic precursors like lactate, pyruvate and alanine. Interestingly, this effect is absent in all other oral anti-diabetic approaches. Lactate is metabolized by the liver and prompts the investigation of liver function (e.g. creatinine levels) in clinical settings. Linagliptin was investigated in rats (up to 30 mg/kg) [U04-1053, Module 4.2.1.2] and it was shown that the majority of measured parameters were unaffected, including osmolarity, creatinine, glucose, BUN, triglycerides, conjugated bilirubin, AST, GGT, GLDH, LDH, ALP, creatine kinase and the electrolytes [Na+], [K+], [Cl-], [Ca2+] and [Mg2+]. In the highest dose (30 mg/kg) serum ALT and total bilirubin were found to be slightly increased.

Based on these parameters linagliptin did not influence liver and other metabolic functions, thus further aggravation with concomitant use with metformin is not expected.

**Metformin** is also known to cause gastro-intestinal side effects like diarrhea, bloating and discomfort. Linagliptin was assessed on its influence on the gastrointestinal system in rats (up to 30 mg/kg) given either intraduodenally [U04-1012, Module 4.2.1.2] or orally [U04-1013, Module 4.2.1.2]. There was no inhibition on the rate of gastric emptying following the administration of linagliptin. A dose-independent slight but significant reduction (about 10%) in the intestinal transit was observed after each dose. The doses of 10 and 30 mg/kg linagliptin led to a slight reduction in gastric juice volume and thereby acid output. The effects on the gastrointestinal tract were in general minimal and point rather to a reduction of intestinal transit than an increased motility. Thus, the combination of linagliptin with metformin is not assumed to aggravate or worsen the side effects of metformin on gastro-intestinal function.

In conclusion, linagliptin, is a very potent, specific and reversible inhibitor of DPP-4 that demonstrates rapid and effective inhibition of DPP-4 activity both *in vitro* and in vivo, which also translates in an excellent anti-diabetic in vivo efficacy. Linagliptin has an excellent safety window when comparing doses required for anti-diabetic efficacy to side effects in the animal models tested. The safety pharmacology assessment of neurological (CNS), cardiovascular and respiratory effects did not identify any significant liabilities of linagliptin. The combination of linagliptin and metformin did result in an additive effect on glucose reduction in diabetic animals. The general pharmacology and safety pharmacology profile of linagliptin, in theory, does not favour an aggravation of the well-known side effects of metformin.
