# 1. PRODUCT DEVELOPMENT RATIONALE

## 1.1 INTRODUCTION AND INTENDED INDICATION

Boehringer Ingelheim has submitted an application for marketing authorisation for linagliptin monotherapy to the European Medicines Agency on 1 July 2010; this application is currently being reviewed. The company is submitting this application for a fixed-dose combination (FDC) of linagliptin/metformin with 3 different dose strengths (2.5 mg/500 mg, 2.5 mg/850 mg, 2.5 mg/1000mg) for the treatment of adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in patients who are inadequately controlled on their maximum tolerated dose of metformin alone or in patients who are already treated with linagliptin and metformin. In addition, the linagliptin/metformin FDC is intended for the treatment of patients inadequately controlled on their maximum tolerated dose of metformin and a sulphonylurea (SU) or in patients already being treated with linagliptin, metformin, and an SU.

## 1.2 MEDICAL BACKGROUND AND DRUG PROFILES

Type 2 diabetes mellitus is a disease with a prevalence of more than 180 million people worldwide, a number expected to rise to 300 million people by the year 2025 [R07-0040, Module 2.7.5]. It is characterised by multiple metabolic dysfunctions involving insulin resistance, impaired insulin secretion, and increased glucose production. T2DM is associated with macrovascular complications (e.g. cardiovascular disease) and microvascular complications (e.g. retinopathy, neuropathy, and nephropathy) [R04-2186, Module 2.7.5].

Linagliptin (BI 1356) is a selective, orally administered, xanthine-based inhibitor of dipepti-dylpeptidase-4 (DPP-4) with a 50% Inhibitor Concentration (IC50) of 1 nM. DPP-4 inhibitors are antidiabetic agents that lower blood glucose by extending the short half life of glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1), which is secreted by intestinal L-cells in response to a meal [P08-07949, Module 2.7.5], and glucose-dependent insulinotropic peptide (GIP), both of which exert glucose-dependent insulinotropic effects and thereby contribute to the maintenance of post-meal glycaemic control. GLP-1 lowers blood glucose by augmenting the glucose-stimulated insulin release and limiting glucagon secretion to slow gastric emptying and to induce satiety. DPP-4 inhibitors maintain long-term β-cell function, which has been demonstrated in animal models. Inhibition of DPP-4 bears little risk of hypoglycaemia in patients with diabetes mellitus because GLP-1 activity ceases when plasma glucose concentration falls below 55 mg/dL. Linagliptin is predominantly excreted unchanged via the faeces. Renal excretion is a minor pathway of elimination of linagliptin at therapeutic doses.

Metformin has been used in Europe for over 50 years and, together with lifestyle modification, is recommended by the European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD) as the first-line treatment for T2DM [R10-4894, Module 2.7.5]. Although its mechanism of action is not yet fully understood, metformin lowers blood glucose levels primarily by suppressing hepatic gluconeogenesis; it is believed that this is achieved through metformin-induced activation of adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase, an energy-regulating enzyme in the liver. Furthermore, metformin improves the insulin sensitivity of peripheral tissues, decreases gastrointestinal tract glucose absorption, and acts as an insulin sensitiser without exerting any direct effect on pancreatic β-cell insulin secretion. Through these mechanisms, metformin therapy typically leads to substantial reductions in glycosylated Hba~1c~ but it does not promote weight gain or increase the risk of hypoglycaemia [R10-4882, R10-4896, Module 2.7.5].

The combination of linagliptin with metformin provides clinically meaningful treatment benefits by lowering glucose and reducing glycosylated haemoglobin (Hba~1c~) further than monotherapy with either component at corresponding doses. The proposed doses of linagliptin/metformin hydrochloride are 2.5 mg/500 mg, 2.5 mg/850 mg, and 2.5 mg/ 1000 mg, all taken twice daily (bid). Combining linagliptin with metformin hydrochloride simplifies the antidiabetic therapy by decreasing the number of tablets to be taken and is expected to improve patients’ compliance with medication; fixed-dose combination therapy has resulted in improved compliance in patients previously treated with oral antidiabetics [R10-1386, R10-1387, Module 2.7.5].

## 1.3 CLINICAL DEVELOPMENT PROGRAMME

Clinical development of linagliptin monotherapy and combination therapy with metformin started in September 2004. The clinical programme that formed the basis for the application for marketing authorisation of linagliptin as monotherapy comprised 24 phase I-trials (among them 9 drug-drug interaction trials), 4 phase II-trials, and 9 large phase III-trials including an open-label extension trial of the 4 pivotal placebo-controlled trials. These studies established the pharmacokinetics, safety, and efficacy profiles of linagliptin monotherapy.

Pharmacokinetics, safety, and efficacy profiles of metformin monotherapy are well established and thoroughly described in the current Summary of Product Characteristics [R10-5341, Module 2.7.5]. For the development of the FDC of linagliptin/metformin, no dedicated studies with metformin monotherapy were conducted. However, information on metformin as monotherapy is included in some of the analysed safety and efficacy groupings in the respective treatment arms.

*Evaluation of efficacy and safety*

The evaluation of efficacy and safety of combination therapy with linagliptin and metformin comprised 14 clinical trials, among them 6 phase I-trials (1288.1, 1288.2, 1288.3, 1288.4, 1218.4, 1218.47), 2 phase II-trials (1218.6, 1218.62), 4 phase III-trials (1218.17, 1218.18, 1218.20, 1218.46), and 2 long-term follow-up trials of the phase III-studies (1218.40, 1218.52). At the time of the database lock for this submission (15 Oct 2010 for all studies but trial 1218.20 for which the database lock was 26 Jan 2011), all studies except the extension studies 1218.40 and 1218.52 had been completed. Data from a small phase I bioequivalence trial in 40 healthy subjects of 2 FDC batches (1288.6, [U11-1379], Module 5.3.1.1) were not included because they were not available at the time of database lock; however the trial report is available. Study 1218.57, which showed bioequivalence between the European (used in study 1218.46) and US Glucophage® reference products, was also conducted as part of the development of the FDC for linagliptin/metformin. This study is not included in the analysis but is discussed in Module 2.7.1 and the trial report [U09-2155, Module 5.3.1.2] is available.

Details on studies and their groupings used to establish efficacy are provided in Section 4.1, details on studies and groupings that form the basis of the safety analysis are provided in Section 5. In total 3529 patients with type 2 diabetes (SAF-C8) and 354 healthy volunteers received treatment with linagliptin and metformin (SAF-C9). Overall 2694 patients were treated for at least 24 weeks, 2081 patients for at least 52 weeks, and 1756 patients for 78 weeks or longer.

In accordance with current regulatory requirements, this considerable exposure forms the basis for the application of marketing authorisation for the combination of linagliptin and metformin. Overall, 43.9% of patients were recruited in Europe, 33.2% in Asia, 11.2% in North America, 10.2% in South America, and 1.5% in Africa.

All trials employed up-to-date methodologies and all trials were conducted according to ICH GCP guidelines subsequent to the review and approval of the relevant ethics committees, institutional review boards, and regulatory authorities. The principal trials were randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group studies and were conducted in a population of male or female patients with type 2 diabetes whose blood concentrations of glycosylated haemoglobin (Hba~1c~) indicated insufficient glycaemic control. All principal trials used the same primary endpoint to assess antidiabetic efficacy: the change in Hba~1c~ from baseline to end of trial. To ensure a homogeneous distribution of patients with poor glycaemic control, randomisation was stratified by the Hba~1c~ level at the beginning of the placebo run-in period (<8.5% versus ≥8.5%) in most of the studies. Randomisation was also stratified by the number of oral antidiabetic drugs (OADs) at the time of enrolment in most of the trials. Also the main secondary endpoints were the same in the entire phase III program, i.e. the change in fasting plasma glucose (FPG) from baseline to end of trial, treat-to-target efficacy response (i.e. Hba~1c~ on treatment <7.0% or <6.5%), and relative efficacy response (i.e. Hba~1c~-lowering ≥0.5%).

*Evaluation of pharmacokinetics*

For the development of the linagliptin/metformin FDC, 6 phase I studies (1288.1, 1288.2, 1288.3, 1288.4, 1288.6, and 1218.57) were performed that established the bioequivalence of the 3 linagliptin/metformin FDC dose strengths and their respective individual components.

In addition, trial 1288.4 [U10-2236, Module 5.3.1.1] investigated the effect of a high fat, high caloric meal on the bioavailability of the Lina 2.5 mg/Metformin 1000 mg FDC. The bioequivalence of 2.5 mg linagliptin twice daily (bid) with 5 mg linagliptin once daily (qd) was established in study 1218.45, and the absence of an interaction between linagliptin and metformin was shown in study 1218.4. Further details are provided in Section 2 and Section 3 below.

## 1.4 INTERACTION WITH REGULATORY AUTHORITIES

During the clinical development of linagliptin and the linagliptin/metformin FDC there was close interaction with key regulatory agencies. For the development of the linagliptin/metformin FDC, scientific advice was sought on 27th May 2008 (EMEA/CHMP/SAWP/ 472394/2008) and final responses were received on 25th September 2008 (EMEA/CHMP/SAWP/472394/2008). This study was subsequently conducted (1218.62), and its results are presented in the current dossier. On 1st July 2010 an application for marketing authorisation of linagliptin monotherapy in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus was submitted to the EMA that is currently being reviewed. This application has received the Day 180 List of Outstanding Issues on 14th April 2011.

On 10th November 2010 a pre-submission meeting for the FDC linagliptin/metformin for the treatment of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus was held with the EMA. It was confirmed that the application is a full-mixed application according to Article 8(3) of Directive 2001/83/EC which requires that the non-clinical and clinical overviews and the summaries of clinical efficacy (SCE) and safety (SCS) cover linagliptin, metformin, and the combination separately, and that each section of the documents must address each of the components. On 9th December 2010 a presubmission meeting with the co-rapporteur the Medicines Evaluation Board of The Netherlands was held. In addition to the endorsement to cover all 3 components (linagliptin, metformin, combination) in the documents separately, it was pointed out that the concerns raised in the list of questions to the linagliptin monotherapy application should be appropriately addressed in the FDC submission. A product-specific paediatric waiver was granted on 11th December 2009 (EMA/802455/2009). On 19th January 2011 an NDA for linagliptin/metformin FDC was filed with the US FDA and is currently under review. On 2 May 2011, Boehringer Ingelheim received approval of the FDA for the use of linagliptin as an adjunct to diet and exercise to improve glycaemic control in adults with T2DM.

# 2. OVERVIEW OF BIOPHARMACEUTICS

For the establishment of efficacy and safety, a film-coated tablet containing 5 mg of linagliptin was developed and characterised in regard to its pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. Evidence of its clinical efficacy and safety was provided by the development program for linagliptin monotherapy.

For the fixed-dose combination, the therapeutic dose of linagliptin of 5 mg qd was split into two doses of 2.5 mg each in order to conform to the twice daily dosing required for metformin. Both linagliptin regimens were shown to be equivalent with regard to DPP-4 inhibition and total steady-state plasma exposure (1218.45 [U08-2132, Module 5.3.1.2]). This was confirmed in regard to clinical efficacy by study 1218.62 [U11-3093, Module 5.3.5.1].

The mono-layer tablet of the intended commercial formulation of linagliptin/metformin was used in the phase I clinical studies 1218.47, 1288.1, 1288.2, 1288.3, and 1288.4. The pivotal study 1218.17, as well as trials 1218.18, 1218.20, and 1218.40 used the free combination of linagliptin 5 mg qd and metformin background therapy in various posologies (total daily dose ≥1500 mg or maximum tolerated dose). Study 1218.62 used the free combination of linagliptin 2.5 mg bid or linagliptin 5 mg qd with metformin background therapy in a bid regimen (total daily dose ≥1500 mg or maximum tolerated dose). Study 1218.46 and its 54-week double-blind extension 1218.52 used the free combination of linagliptin 2.5 mg bid and metformin 500 mg bid or 1000 mg bid.

Bioequivalence between the fixed-dose formulations and the free combinations was established in studies 1288.1, 1288.2, and 1288.3; details are available in [Modules 2.7.1 and 2.7.2]. In the bioequivalence trials and in studies 1218.46 and 1218.52 the European metformin reference product was supplied as part of the study medication (Glucophage® Merck Serono). In the pivotal trial 1218.17 as well as in studies 1218.18, 1218.20, 1218.40,  and 1218.62, patients continued with their local prescribed metformin tablets, i.e. metformin was not supplied by Boehringer Ingelheim as part of the study medication.

The relative bioavailability of linagliptin was not relevantly affected when the FDC tablet was administered under fed conditions (study 1288.4). Metformin extent of absorption was not relevantly altered, whereas maximum concentrations were reduced by about 18% when given with food. The decrease in the rate and extent of absorption of metformin under fed conditions, seen in this study, was less pronounced than previously reported for Glucophage®, but is well within the range of food-effects on other FDC tablets containing metformin; e.g., Actoplus Met®, Avandamet®, Eucreas®, and Metaglip® [R10-4454, R10-4455, R10-4456, and R10-4457, Module 2.7.5]. Because metformin is recommended to be given with meals to reduce gastrointestinal side effects, the FDC tablet is also proposed to be given with food. Single oral doses of 2.5 mg linagliptin/1000 mg metformin were safe and well tolerated when administered with and without food (1288.4).

# 3. OVERVIEW OF CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY

Linagliptin

The pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of linagliptin were evaluated in healthy subjects and patients with T2DM. In these studies, linagliptin was administered as a single oral dose up to 600 mg or multiple doses every 24 hours up to 10 mg/day. The studies were designed to address issues related to linagliptin single-dose and multiple-dose pharmacokinetics, dose proportionality, excretion/mass balance, metabolite identification, effects of food on bioavailability, pharmacodynamics, drug interactions, pharmacokinetics in special populations (gender, race, BMI/weight, elderly, and renal or hepatic impairment) and absolute/relative bioavailability. Details are available in Module 2.7.2.

There is no need for dose adjustments of linagliptin on the basis of age, gender, BMI, race, or renal or hepatic impairment. Linagliptin had no clinically relevant effect on the pharmacokinetics of metformin, glibenclamide, simvastatin, pioglitazone, warfarin, digoxin, or oral contraceptives. These findings constitute in vivo evidence of a low propensity for drug interactions with substrates of CYP3A4, CYP2C9, CYP2C8, P-glycoprotein, and organic cationic transporters (OCT).

Co-administration of a single 5 mg oral dose of linagliptin and multiple 200 mg oral doses of ritonavir, a potent inhibitor of P-gp and CYP3A4, increased the AUC and Cmax of linagliptin approximately 2-fold and 3-fold. A likely explanation for this observation is an increase in the bioavailability of linagliptin due to an inhibition of P-gp. As ritonavir is one of the most potent P-gp/CYP3A4 inhibitors, clinically relevant interactions with weaker P-gp/CYP3A4 inhibitors are not expected. Furthermore, since linagliptin has a broad safety window, as demonstrated by tolerability of single doses of up to 600 mg, and a safety margin of at least 40-fold based on data from toxicology studies, the increase in AUC and Cmax is not of clinical relevance.

Conversely, multiple co-administrations of linagliptin with rifampicin, a potent inducer of P-gp and CYP3A4, resulted in a decrease of about 40% in linagliptin steady-state exposure and a decrease of about 30% in DPP-4 inhibition. This observation is most likely due to an inductive effect on P-gp. Thus, linagliptin in combination with strong P-gp inducers might not reach full clinical efficacy.

Metformin

No studies investigating the possible influence of intrinsic or extrinsic factors on metformin pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics were performed because all relevant information is available in the scientific literature and in the SPC [R10-5537, R11-0761, Module 2.7.5].

Patients taking metformin should be closely observed for loss of blood glucose control when the following medications are taken concomitantly: thiazides and other diuretics, corticosteroids, phenothiazines, thyroid products, estrogens, oral contraceptives, phenytoin, nicotinic acid, sympathomimetics, calcium-channel-blocking drugs, and isoniazid.

For metformin no dosage adjustment is recommended based on age, gender, BMI, or race.

However, metformin treatment should not be initiated in patients 80 years of age or older unless measurement of creatinine clearance demonstrates that renal function is not reduced.

Metformin is contraindicated in patients with renal failure and renal dysfunction even if moderate (creatinine clearance <60 mL/min).

Linagliptin/metformin combination

A possible interaction of linagliptin and metformin was investigated in study 1218.4. It showed that co-administration of linagliptin and metformin did not have a clinically meaningful effect on linagliptin pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. Also, linagliptin did not meaningfully affect metformin exposure.

No dedicated studies were performed that investigated the effect of intrinsic or extrinsic factors on the pharmacokinetics of the linagliptin/metformin FDC formulation. However, it can be assumed that the same conditions, limitations, and dose adjustments as reported for the single entities will apply to the fixed-dose single-tablet combination. Hence, for the linagliptin/metformin FDC, no dosage adjustment is necessary based on age, gender, BMI/weight, or race. However, due to the metformin component, treatment with the combination should generally be avoided in hepatically impaired patients since hepatic impairment has been associated rarely with lactic acidosis. The FDC is also contraindicated in patients with renal impairment (creatinine clearance <60 mL/min).

## 3.1 PHARMACOKINETICS

### 3.1.1 Linagliptin

The pharmacokinetics of linagliptin have been extensively characterised in healthy subjects and patients with T2DM. After oral administration of a single 5 mg dose to healthy subjects, linagliptin was rapidly absorbed, with peak plasma concentrations (Tmax) occurring 1.5 hour post dose; the gMean plasma AUC0-24 was 139 nmol•h/L (gCV 25.9%) and gMean Cmax was 8.90 nmol/L (gCV 40.3%) [1218.25, U07-2003, Module 5.3.1.2].

Plasma concentrations of linagliptin decline in a triphasic manner with a long terminal half life (>100 h) related to the saturable, tight binding of linagliptin to DPP-4. The prolonged elimination phase does not contribute to the accumulation of the drug. The effective half-life for accumulation of linagliptin, as determined from oral administration of multiple doses of linagliptin 5 mg, is approximately 12 hours. After once-daily dosing, steady-state plasma concentrations of linagliptin 5 mg are reached by the third dose, and Cmax and AUC increased by a factor of about 1.3 at steady-state compared to the first dose. The intra-subject and inter-subject coefficients of variation for linagliptin steady-state AUC were small (12.6% and 28.5%, respectively). Plasma AUC of linagliptin increased in a less than dose proportional manner. The pharmacokinetics of linagliptin were similar in healthy subjects and in patients with T2DM. Bioequivalence of 5 mg linagliptin once daily compared with 2.5 mg linagliptin twice daily was shown for 24-hour steady-state AUC in study 1218.45.

[Absorption:]{.underline} The absolute bioavailability of linagliptin is approximately 30%. Because co-administration with a high-fat meal had no clinically relevant effect on the pharmacokinetics, linagliptin itself may be administered with or without food.

[Distribution:{.underline} The mean apparent volume of distribution at steady state following a single intravenous dose of linagliptin 5 mg to healthy subjects was approximately 1110 L, indicating that linagliptin extensively distributes into the tissues. Plasma protein binding of linagliptin is concentration dependent, decreasing from about 99% at 1 nmol/L to 75-89% at ≥30 nmol/L, reflecting saturation of binding to DPP-4 with increasing concentration of linagliptin. At high concentrations, where DPP-4 is fully saturated, 70-80% of linagliptin was bound to plasma proteins other than DPP-4; hence, 20-30% was unbound in plasma. Plasma protein binding was not altered in patients with renal or hepatic impairment.

[Metabolism:{.underline} Following oral administration, the majority (about 90%) of linagliptin was excreted unchanged, indicating that metabolism represents a minor elimination pathway. A small fraction of absorbed linagliptin is metabolized by CYP3A4 to a pharmacologically inactive metabolite (CD 1790), which shows a steady-state exposure of 13.3% relative to linagliptin and is predominantly eliminated non-renally.

[Excretion:{.underline} Linagliptin is primarily eliminated unchanged via the faeces. Following administration of an oral [14C]-linagliptin dose to healthy subjects, approximately 85% of the administered radioactivity was eliminated via the faeces (80%) or urine (5%) within 4 days of dosing. Renal clearance at steady state was approximately 70 mL/min.

*Special populations*

Results of individual studies and population pharmacokinetic analyses of data from phase II trials indicate that no dosage adjustments are required because of renal or hepatic impairment, gender, weight/Body Mass Index (BMI), race, ethnicity, or age. Studies characterizing the pharmacokinetics of linagliptin in paediatric patients are ongoing (1218.56).

### 3.1.2 Metformin

No studies dedicated to the evaluation of pharmacokinetics of metformin were conducted in the context of the linagliptin/metformin FDC; therefore information of pharmacokinetics is derived from the SPC [R10-5537, R11-0761; Module 2.7.5].

[Absorption and bioavailability:{.underline} After an oral dose of metformin, Tmax is reached in 2.5 hours.

The absolute bioavailability is approximately 50% to 60%. After oral administration, metformin hydrochloride absorption is saturable and therefore metformin pharmacokinetics is non-linear. At the usual metformin doses and dosing schedules, steady state plasma concentrations are reached within 24 to 48 hours. Food decreases the extent of and slightly delays the absorption of metformin as shown by an approximately 40% lower Cmax, a 25% lower AUC, and a 35-minute prolongation of Tmax following administration of a single 850 mg tablet. However, the clinical relevance of these changes is unknown.

[Distribution:{.underline} Plasma protein binding is negligible. Metformin partitions into erythrocytes and the blood peak is lower than the plasma peak and appears at approximately the same time; the mean volume of distribution (Vd) was between 63-276 L.

[Metabolism and elimination:{.underline} Metformin is excreted unchanged in the urine; no metabolites have been identified in humans. The renal clearance of metformin is greater than 400 mL/min, indicating that metformin is eliminated by glomerular filtration and tubular secretion. Following an oral dose, the apparent terminal elimination half-life is approximately 6.5 hours.

*Special populations*

[Renal impairment:{.underline} In patients with decreased renal function, the plasma and blood half-life of metformin is prolonged and its renal clearance is decreased in proportion to the decrease in creatinine clearance, leading to higher levels of metformin in the plasma. Metformin is contraindicated in acute conditions that have a potential to alter renal function such as dehydration, severe infection, shock, or patients receiving an intravascular administration of iodinated contrast agents.

[Hepatic impairment:{.underline} No studies in patients with hepatic impairment were performed for the linagliptin/metformin FDC development because metformin is contraindicated in these patients.

[Gender and race:{.underline} Although dedicated studies to study the influence of gender or race are not available, there are no indications that the pharmacokinetics and clinical efficacy of metformin are substantially influenced by these factors.

[Contraindications:{.underline} Metformin is contraindicated in patients with renal impairment or hepatic insufficiency, patients who suffer from acute alcohol intoxication, or patients with alcoholism. Metformin is also contraindicated in patients with an acute or chronic disease which may cause tissue hypoxia such as cardiac or respiratory failure, recent myocardial infarction, or shock and also during lactation. Patients with a history of lactic acidosis must not take metformin.

*Drug interactions with metformin*

Drug interactions of metformin were reported for glyburide, furosemide, nifedipine, and are likely for cationic drugs that are eliminated by renal tubular secretion (e.g. amiloride, cimetidine, digoxin, morphine, procainamide, quinidine, quinine, ranitidine, triamterene, trimethoprim, or vancomycin). Certain drugs tend to produce hyperglycaemia and may cause loss of glycaemic control. These drugs include the thiazides and other diuretics, corticosteroids, phenothiazines, thyroid products, estrogens, oral contraceptives, phenytoin, nicotinic acid, sympathomimetics, calcium-channel-blocking drugs, and isoniazid. In healthy volunteers, the pharmacokinetics of metformin and propranolol, and metformin and ibuprofen were not affected when co-administered in single-dose interaction studies. Metformin is negligibly bound to plasma proteins and is, therefore, less likely to interact with highly protein-bound drugs such as salicylates, sulfonamides, chloramphenicol, and probenecid, as compared to the sulfonylureas, which are extensively bound to serum proteins. Detailed information on the magnitude of the interaction effects is available in the scientific literature [P10-09483, R10-2970, Module 2.7.5].

# 4. OVERVIEW OF EFFICACY

## 4.1 EFFICACY OF LINAGLIPTIN MONOTHERAPY

### 4.1.1 Clinical trial programme

The clinical programme for linagliptin as monotherapy started in September 2004 and comprised 14 phase II/III trials; among them 4 studies that investigated the efficacy of linagliptin as monotherapy versus placebo medication. The pivotal trial for linagliptin monotherapy was study 1218.16, a phase III-trial that investigated the efficacy of linagliptin 5 mg once daily versus placebo over 24 weeks. Supportive evidence is derived from a 12-week phase II-trial (1218.5), a 12-week phase III-trial in Japanese patients (1218.23), and an 18-week phase III-trial in patients ineligible for treatment with metformin (1218.50). These 4 studies formed the EFF-4 grouping whose data were submitted in June 2010 to support a linagliptin monotherapy registration.

Further evidence is available from study 1218.46, which included a comparison of linagliptin 5 mg qd with placebo over 24 weeks in a partial first-line therapy setting. These data are presented in the section on the trials that support linagliptin/metformin combination (Section 4.1.3). Long-term efficacy of linagliptin monotherapy was investigated by combining data from patients who participated in study 1218.16 and continued treatment in the open-label extension study 1218.40. The data of the 2 trials are combined to form efficacy grouping EFF-M1 (“M” denotes linagliptin monotherapy).

### 4.1.2 Disposition, demographics, and baseline characteristics

The durations of the trials included in EFF-4 varied from 12 to 24 weeks (see above). The percentage of patients who completed the planned treatment duration was above 90% in all studies but trial 1218.5 (74.4%). The frequencies of premature discontinuations were lower with linagliptin than with placebo in all studies. The most frequent reasons for premature discontinuation were either lack of efficacy, occurrence of adverse events, or administrative issues.

Of the patients who had completed the initial study 1218.16, almost all (94.3%) continued treatment in the open-label extension study 1218.40. In study 1218.40, all patients received linagliptin 5 mg, i.e. patients who had received placebo in study 1218.16 were switched to linagliptin 5 mg. Overall, 90.1% of the patients from study 1218.16 did not prematurely discontinue treatment in study 1218.40, with similar frequencies in those patients who had previously received linagliptin (88.9%) or placebo (92.5%). Premature discontinuations occurred most frequently for administrative reasons (3.4%) and the occurrence of adverse events (2.9%). Further details on the disposition of patients in EFF-M1 are available in the SCE [Module 2.7.3, Section 3.1.1.1].

The mean age was similar in the 4 studies in EFF-4 (55 to 60 years); the proportion of male patients ranged from about 36% (1218.50) to about 70% (1218.23). Due to the different locations of the studies, the percentages of White, Black and Asian patients varied. The majority of patients were White in all studies except study 1218.23, which comprised only Asian patients. Consequently, the mean BMI in study 1218.23 was lower than in the other studies in EFF-4.

Mean baseline Hba~1c~ and mean FPG concentrations were similar across studies in EFF-4. The percentage of patients diagnosed with diabetes for more than 5 years varied between trials (20% to 50%), but was similar between treatment groups in all studies. About half of the patients in all 4 studies had not been pre-treated with antidiabetic drugs. Most of those with pre-treatment, had previously received 1 OAD; 10% or less in all trials had received 2 or more OADs. The baseline characteristics of the patients from study 1218.16 who continued treatment in the open-label extension study 1218.40 (EFF-M1) were very similar to the baseline characteristics of all patients in study 1218.16. Further details on disposition, demographics and baseline characteristics are available in the SCE [Module 2.7.3, Section 3.1].

## 4.3 EFFICACY OF COMBINATION THERAPY WITH LINAGLIPTIN AND METFORMIN

### 4.3.1 Clinical trial programme for combination therapy with linagliptin and metformin

Overall, 7 trials contributed to the demonstration of efficacy of the combination therapy with linagliptin and metformin. The pivotal placebo-controlled phase III-trial 1218.17 investigated the efficacy of linagliptin as add-on therapy to metformin over 24 weeks. Additional key trials for the establishment of efficacy were studies 1218.62 and 1218.20. The purpose of trial 1218.62, a placebo-controlled bridging study over 12 weeks, was to show comparable efficacy of the linagliptin 2.5 mg twice daily (bid) regimen and the linagliptin 5 mg qd regimen when added to metformin. Trial 1218.20 was an active-controlled phase III-trial that compared linagliptin 5 mg qd with glimepiride as add-on therapy to metformin over 2 years. These 3 key trials were supported by the phase III-study 1218.46, which tested the combination of linagliptin and metformin in a partial first-line setting, i.e. patients were either treatment-naïve (47.5%) or washed out one previously taken oral antidiabetic agent before randomisation.

Efficacy of linagliptin in combination with metformin and an SU over 24 weeks was investigated in the phase III-study 1218.18, where linagliptin 5 mg qd or placebo were added to ongoing therapy with metformin and an SU.

Long-term efficacy data are available from 2 extension trials. Study 1218.40 is an open-label extension trial in patients who completed the treatment periods of the 4 pivotal placebo/ background-controlled studies from the linagliptin mono programme. To show long-term efficacy of the combination therapy with linagliptin and metformin (and SU), data from the patients who completed studies 1218.17 or 1218.18 and continued in study 1218.40 are presented. Study 1218.52 is a double-blind metformin monotherapy-controlled extension trial in patients who completed the treatment period of study 1218.46 without requiring rescue medication. From studies 1218.20 and 1218.40, interim data were presented for the linagliptin mono submission. In this FDC submission, final (i.e. 2-year) data from study 1218.20 are included, and a new interim data cut-off was performed for study 1218.40. Not included in the evaluation of efficacy is the phase II study 1218.6, a 12-week dose-finding trial, which tested linagliptin qd doses of 1 mg, 5 mg, and 10 mg versus placebo as add-on therapy to metformin. The trial included 66 patients treated with linagliptin 5 mg qd and it was felt that the contribution to the overall evidence would be small, particular since the treatment duration was just 12 weeks. However, the results were consistent with other trials. The difference to placebo in adjusted mean change from baseline (SE) in Hba~1c~ was -0.73% (0.14), p<0.0001; for FPG the adjusted mean change was -34.7 mg/dL (6.2); further details are in the study report [U08-1056, Module 5.3.5.1].

### 4.3.2 Disposition, demographics, and baseline characteristics

Studies conducted for the linagliptin/metformin FDC programme as well as studies conducted as part of the linagliptin monotherapy programme were considered for the evaluation of the efficacy of combination therapy with linagliptin and metformin. Long-term efficacy was assessed by combining the parent studies 1218.17 and 1218.18 with the open-label extension trial 1218.40 to reach treatment durations of up to 102 weeks (EFF-C8 and EFF-C10). Additional information on long-term treatment is available from study 1218.46 combined with its double-blind extension study 1218.52 for treatments of up to 78 weeks (EFF-C9). In the following section disposition information is based on the FAS sets.

The durations of the combination therapy trials ranged from 12 weeks (1218.62) to 104 weeks (1218.20). The percentage of randomised patients who completed the planned treatment time was above 90% in all trials except study 1218.20 (78.4%), possibly because of the longer duration of the trial. The percentages of premature discontinuation in the Lina+Met groups were below 7.5% in all placebo-controlled trials and the incidences were generally lower in the Lina+Met groups than in the Met groups except for study 1218.62. The most frequent reasons for premature discontinuations in the Lina+Met groups were administrative reasons (1.4% to 4.4% across trials) and adverse events (0.9% to 2.5% across trials).

In the combined studies for long-term efficacy assessment, 85.0% or more of the patients did not prematurely discontinue their study treatment. In the extensions of trials 1218.17 (EFF-C8) and 1218.18 (EFF-C10), the frequencies of premature discontinuations were between 10.5% and 12.9% across treatment groups. The discontinuation rates were comparable between those patients who had received linagliptin already in the initial trial and those patients who had switched to linagliptin with the entry in the follow-up trial. In the combined 1218.46 and 1218.52 studies (EFF-C9), the percentage of patients who discontinued prematurely were in a similar range with 7.3% (Met1000 bid), 10.8% (Lina2.5 mg+Met1000 bid), and 15.0% (Lina2.5 mg+Met 500).

The key demographic characteristics of patients were balanced between treatment groups for the studies that investigated the efficacy of linagliptin and metformin combination therapy. Across these studies, the mean age of patients was similar (range 55.2 to 59.9 years). The percentage of male patients varied between 47.2% and 60.1%. In all studies but 1218.18, the majority of patients were of White race, with the greatest percentage of White patients in study 1218.20 (84.7%). In study 1218.18, 52.7% of patients were of Asian race. As a consequence of the different race composition, the total mean BMI was lowest in study 1218.18 (28.3 kg/m2) and highest in study 1218.20 (30.3 kg/m2).

Mean baseline Hba~1c~ was in the range of 7.7% to 8.7%; mean FPG at baseline was between 158.7 mg/dL and 203.7 mg/dL. The percentages of patients diagnosed with diabetes for more than 5 years differed between trials (19.6% to 73.9%). In studies 1218.17 (EFF-C1), 1218.20 (EFF-C2), and 1218.62 (EFF-C7a), about 50% of patients had been diagnosed with diabetes for more than 5 years. Within the individual studies, the key baseline characteristics were balanced between the treatment groups except for the lower proportion of patients diagnosed with diabetes for more than 5 years in study 1218.46.

*Additional evidence for the efficacy of combination therapy with linagliptin and metformin*

Additional evidence for the efficacy of combination therapy with Lina+Met is derived from 3 large trials: study 1218.62 compared Lina 2.5 mg bid with Lina 5 mg qd and with placebo as add-on to metformin over 12 weeks, study 1218.46 evaluated 2 different dose combinations of Lina and Met over 24 weeks, and study 1218.20 tested Lina 5 mg qd versus glimepiride (‘Glim’) 1 to 4 mg qd over 104 weeks on a background of metformin. The analyses presented in this section were not stratified for metformin dose or for metformin posology; analyses by metformin dose are available in Section 4.4.3.

Study 1218.62 had a non-inferiority design that employed a hierarchical testing procedure.  Step 1 was a test for superiority of Lina 2.5 mg bid over placebo; if successful, testing continued (Step 2) with a non-inferiority test of Lina 2.5 mg bid versus Lina 5 mg qd using a pre-specified non-inferiority margin of 0.35%. If successful, testing concluded in Step 3 with a test for superiority of Lina 5 mg qd over placebo. The adjusted mean treatment difference in Hba~1c~ change from baseline to Week 12 for Lina 2.5 bid over placebo was -0.74% (95% CI -0.97, -0.52), p<0.0001. The adjusted mean treatment difference for Lina 2.5 mg bid vs. Lina 5 mg qd was 0.06% (95% CI -0.07, 0.19), indicating non-inferiority of Lina 2.5 mg bid to Lina 5 mg qd as the upper bound of the 95% confidence interval was below the prespecified margin of 0.35%. For Lina 5 mg over placebo the adjusted mean difference was -0.80% (95% CI -1.02, -0.58), p<0.0001.

Study 1218.46 was conducted in a first-line setting (47.5% treatment-naïve patients) and yielded qualitatively similar results to the pivotal trial. The Lina+Met groups were superior to both Met monotherapy groups and also to linagliptin monotherapy with p-values below 0.0001.

Across all studies, including the pivotal trial 1218.17, the additional reductions in Hba~1c~ achieved with combination therapies with linagliptin and metformin over metformin monotherapy (as background or as study treatment) were in the range of 0.51% to 0.64%.
Hence all trials demonstrated a clinically meaningful reduction in Hba~1c~ with combination therapy of linagliptin and metformin.

Study 1218.20 was also designed as a non-inferiority trial. After 104 weeks of treatment, 4 confirmatory hypotheses were to be tested sequentially at α=0.025 (2-sided; alpha-adjustment due to interim analysis after 52 weeks): (1) non-inferiority of Lina versus glimepiride in the change in Hba~1c~ from baseline (pre-defined non-inferiority margin: 0.35%), (2) superiority of Lina over glimepiride for the change in Hba~1c~ from baseline, (3) superiority of Lina over glimepiride for the change in body weight from baseline, and (4) superiority of Lina over glimepiride in the occurrence of hypoglycaemic events. After 104 weeks of treatment, a mean Hba~1c~ decrease from baseline was observed in both treatment groups (Lina+Met -0.16% vs. Glim+Met -0.36%; adjusted mean difference 0.20%). The 2-sided 97.5% CI (0.09%, 0.299%) for the treatment difference excluded both the pre-specified non-inferiority margin of 0.35% and the stricter margin of 0.30%. Thus, Lina+Met was non-inferior to Glim+Met. This notwithstanding, superiority of linagliptin over glimepiride could not be shown. Therefore, the remaining 2 hypotheses of the sequential testing procedure could not be tested in a confirmatory way. A number of sensitivity analyses were performed for the primary endpoint of Hba~1c~ change from baseline. The results of the sensitivity analyses were concordant with the results for the primary analysis as the 97.5% CIs excluded the non-inferiority margin of 0.35%. Only in the PPS-LOCF-analysis (n=1160), which excluded patients with protocol violations that possibly impacted on the efficacy outcomes, the upper bound of the 97.5% CI was above the margin (0.40%). However, a more pertinent analysis concentrating on longterm efficacy and therefore based on the PPS-completers (OC, n=905)), i.e. those patients in the PPS that were treated with study medication for at least 684 days, resulted in a 97.5% CI (0.07, 0.28) with the upper bound being well below the non-inferiority margin of 0.35% as well as below 0.30%. #### 4.3.3.2 Changes from baseline in FPG

The change from baseline in FPG was a secondary endpoint in all trials. Combination therapy with Lina+Met was more efficacious in the reduction of FPG than metformin monotherapy (background or study treatment) and linagliptin monotherapy. In study 1218.46, the reductions in FPG were similar in both Lina+Met combination therapy groups and were larger than in the respective metformin groups. Also, as for the Hba~1c~ change from baseline, both combination therapies were more efficacious in lowering FPG than Lina 5 mg once daily. Thus the analysis of the mean change from baseline in FPG corroborates the results observed for Hba~1c~, providing further evidence for the efficacy of combination therapies of linagliptin + metformin.

In study 1218.20, consistent with the Hba~1c~ decrease from baseline, a mean reduction in FPG after 104 weeks was observed in both treatment groups. The mean decrease in FPG was less pronounced for Lina+Met than for Glim+Met, with a treatment difference in the adjusted mean FPG change from baseline of 6.4 mg/dL (p=0.0012). Lina+Met provided a smaller reduction in FPG levels than Glim+Met after 104 weeks of treatment. #### 4.3.3.4 Other measures of efficacy: Post-prandial glucose (2hPPG), use of rescue therapy, body weight, and HOMA indices

For the pivotal trial 1218.17, the reduction in post-prandial glucose levels (2hPPG) showed a significant difference in favour of combination therapy of Lina+Met over Met background therapy (adjusted mean 2hPPG change -67.1 mg/dL; 95% CI -94.7, -39.6; p <0.0001). Postprandial glucose levels were also measured in trial 1218.46 but due to the small number of patients in the placebo group in the MTT-set in this study, the data is inconclusive.

In study 1218.20, the adjusted mean change from baseline in 2hPPG at Week 104 was numerically larger for patients receiving Lina+Met (-28.5 mg/dL) than for patients receiving Glim+Met (-18.7 mg/dL), even though the treatment difference of -9.7 mg/dL was not statistically significant (95% CI -21.1, 1.6; p=0.0918).

In the pivotal trial 1218.17, the use of rescue therapy was substantially lower in the Lina+Met group than in the Met group (7.8% vs. 18.9%). This was also consistently observed in the other trials: the use of rescue medication was always lower in the combination therapy groups than in monotherapy groups. This agrees with the analysis of patients achieving glycaemic control where the combination therapy groups exhibited higher proportions of patients achieving Hba~1c~ levels below 7% or 6.5% (Figure 4.3.3.3: 1, above).

In study 1218.17, the effect of combination therapy was shown to be neutral in regard to body weight, since the mean body weight decreased by 0.4 kg in both treatment groups over the 24 weeks of treatment. Also in the other trials only minor differences between the combination therapy groups and metformin mono therapy were observed.

In the long-term active-controlled study 1218.20, after 104 weeks of treatment, a decrease in body weight was noted in the Lina+Met group as opposed to a weight gain in the Glim+Met group (adjusted mean changes -1.4 kg vs. +1.3 kg). Thus, a clinically meaningful treatment difference of -2.7 kg (p <0.0001) was observed between the Lina+Met and Glim+Met groups.

There was no difference in study 1218.17 between Lina+Met and Met in regards to the change in insulin resistance (HOMA-IR). The evaluation of HOMA-%B indicated an improvement in ß-cell function in patients receiving Lina+Met (mean change from baseline 1.21 mU/mmol) but not in those receiving metformin (-10.67 mU/mmol); p=0.1177.

#### 4.3.3.5 Efficacy in subpopulations

To obtain reasonable group sizes for the evaluation of subgroups, the 24-week trials 1218.17 and 1218.46 were pooled (EFF-C5, n=1244); the analysis was confined to the change from baseline in Hba~1c~. Data on disposition, demographics and baseline characteristics in subgroups are available in the SCE [Module 2.7.3, Section 3.1.3].

For most subgroups investigated, the treatment effect was consistent and the achieved changes from baseline in Hba~1c~ were comparable across all subcategories (Table 4.3.3.5: 1). Thus, the factors age, gender, ethnicity, geographical region, baseline BMI, and baseline Hba~1c~ did not have an influence on the efficacy of combination therapy with Lina+Met. The treatment effect of Lina+Met compared with Met was almost identical in Europe (n=442) and in Asia (n=446) with Hba~1c~ changes of -0.56% (Europe) and -0.59% (Asia), and p-values below 0.0001. Likewise, for race there was no difference between White (-0.61%) and Asian (-0.57%) patients in the efficacy of Lina+Met compared with Met (p<0.0001 for both races).

## 4.4 DOSE RESPONSE AND DOSING RATIONALE

### 4.4.1 Dosing rationale for linagliptin monotherapy

The optimum daily linagliptin dose of 5 mg was determined in the linagliptin mono development programme and was primarily based on Hba~1c~ reduction and DPP-4 inhibition in the 2 dose-finding trials 1218.5 [U08-3761, Module 5.3.5.1] and 1218.6 [U08-1056, Module 5.3.5.1]. Tolerability was excellent on all dose levels tested in these 2 trials (0.5 mg, 1 mg, 2.5 mg, 5 mg, and 10 mg), and no dose-dependent increase in adverse events was observed. A median DPP-4 inhibition of at least 80% was only reached with the 5 mg and 10 mg linagliptin doses, but not with lower doses. The 10 mg dose did not result in a substantially greater median DPP-4 inhibition or Hba~1c~ reduction than the 5 mg dose, and hence, the 5 mg dose was used in phase III. In the entire phase III-programme, linagliptin 5 mg once daily was shown to provide consistent and clinically meaningful improvements in glycaemic control, as asssessed by Hba~1c~, FPG, or postprandial glucose levels. Together with the favourable safety profile observed, the risk-benefit ratio of linagliptin is considered optimal for a daily dose of 5 mg linagliptin.

Because the pharmacokinetics of metformin require an at least twice daily dosing, for the development of the FDC the once daily dosing of 5 mg linagliptin was split into 2 daily doses of 2.5 mg. In trial 1218.45, a phase I-study comparing once daily and twice daily dosing in healthy subjects, the 2 linagliptin dosing regimens were shown to be bioequivalent in regard to AUC at steady state; the DPP-4 inhibition levels reached with both posologies were also similar [U08-2132, Module 5.3.1.2]. The clinical equivalence of both posologies was demonstrated in a large, placebo-controlled, 12-week trial 1218.62 that investigated Lina 2.5 mg bid+Met vs. Lina 5 mg qd + Met. Details on trial 1218.62 are available in Section 4.3 above.

### 4.4.2 Dosing of metformin

Metformin in immediate release formulation is approved as tablet for oral administration in the dose strengths of 500 mg, 850 mg, and 1000 mg [R10-5341, Module 2.7.5]. In case of tolerability issues, the metformin dose should be reduced. It is therefore proposed to provide FDC tablets with linagliptin and all 3 metformin dose strenghts to offer patients maximal flexibility in attaining their individual daily metformin dose.

For the key trials in this submission (1218.17, 1218.20, 1218.62, and 1218.18) the protocols specified that the daily metformin dose should be 1500 mg or above. The actual unit dose strength and the actual posology was at the investigator’s descretion and depending on the patient’s need and tolerability of metformin. The majority of patients in the 4 studies received a daily dose of 1500 mg to 2000 mg metformin at baseline; a considerable proportion of patients took took less than 1500 mg metformin per day (range 6.3% to 8.6%). In trial 1218.46, two treatment groups with metformin mono therapy of 500 mg and 1000 mg bid were evaluated.

### 4.4.3 Dosing of combination therapy with linagliptin and metformin

The applicant proposes 3 FDC dose strengths, namely Lina 2.5 mg/Met 500 mg, Lina 2.5 mg /Met 850 mg, and Lina 2.5 mg/Met 1000 mg. ## 4.5 PERSISTENCE OF EFFICACY

This section seperately describes the persistence of efficacy of linagliptin monotherapy, metformin monotherapy, and combination therapy with linagliptin and metformin. The key evidence for persistent efficacy is derived by combining data from the 24-week trials 1218.16 (linagliptin monotherapy) and 1218.17 (linagliptin + metformin combination) with their open-label extension 1218.40. Note that these analyses were based on observed cases (OC),  i.e. missing values were not imputed, and values measured after a patient had received rescue medication were set to missing.

Furthermore, persistence of the initial combination of linagliptin + metformin is derived from a combination of data from the 24-week study 1218.46 and its double-blind 54-week extension 1218.52. The 2-year data of the double-blind active-controlled trial 1218.20 provides evidence of the effectiveness of linagliptin + metformin for 102 weeks. Persistent efficacy for the triple combination of linagliptin + metformin + SU is demonstrated by combining the data of study 1218.18 with the open-label extension 1218.40.

Generally, the study protocols of the initial studies 1218.16, .17, and .18 and their follow-up trial 1218.40 made identical provisions in regard to inclusion/exclusion criteria, compliance with study medication, reporting of safety data etc. However, the criteria for the use of rescue differed slightly. In the last 12 weeks of the study, patients were to receive rescue medication when their FPG levels were above 200 mg/dL; during the last 72 weeks of the follow-up trial, patients were to receive rescue medication if their FPG was above 180 mg/dL. This lower threshold for initiation of rescue medication might have inflated the proportion of patients receiving rescue medication in the follow-up trial. For the extension study 1218.40, all patients completing the initial trials were eligible; however, study 1218.52 included only patients completing the 1218.46 trial without requiring rescue medication. Therefore, a comparison between the 2 extension trials in regard to the use of rescue therapy has to be done with caution.

### 4.5.1 Persistence of efficacy of linagliptin monotherapy

To evaluate the persistence of linagliptin monotherapy, data from the placebo-controlled 24-week study 1218.16 were combined with interim data from its 78-week open-label extension study 1218.40 to provide data over up to 102 weeks of treatment (study grouping EFF-M1).

Patients who had received placebo in the initial trial received linagliptin 5 mg qd in the follow-up trial (‘Pbo/Lina5’), patients who had received linagliptin in the initial trial continued with this treatment (‘Lina 5 mg’). The frequency of premature discontinuations was low in both arms (Pbo/Lina5: 7.5%; Lina 5 mg: 11.1%) and the median treatment duration was 721 and 720 days, respectively. Overall exposure was 279.7 patient years in the Pbo/Lina5 group and 552.3 patient years in the Lina 5 mg group. The demographic characteristics and baseline conditions were similar in the initial trial and the follow-up study.

*Change from baseline in Hba~1c~ and FPG over time in the combined studies 1218.16 and 1218.40 (EFF-M1)*

At the end of the 24-week treatment period of study 1218.16, the mean change from baseline in Hba~1c~ in the patients who had received linagliptin in study 1218.16 was -0.55%; after 102 weeks, the mean change from baseline was -0.48%. Likewise for FPG, patients who had received linagliptin in study 1218.16 had a mean FPG change of -10.3 mg/dL after 24 weeks and of -9.0 mg/dL after 102 weeks. Thus, the analysis of the FPG change over time in the combined studies 1218.16/1218.40 confirmed the results obtained for the Hba~1c~ change, suggesting that the glucose-lowering effect of linagliptin monotherapy was sustained over up to 102 weeks.

### 4.5.2 Persistence of efficacy of metformin monotherapy

Initial improvements in Hba~1c~ achieved with metformin monotherapy are generally lost after approximately 5 years. Patients who initially respond well to metformin monotherapy often require combination therapy to maintain long-term glycaemic control. In some patients, initial metformin monotherapy fails to produce sufficient reductions in Hba~1c~ and FPG; in these cases, add-on therapy is necessary [R10-4896, Module 2.7.5].

From the clinical development programme for combination therapy with linagliptin and metformin, long-term efficacy data for metformin monotherapy are available from study grouping EFF-C9. For this study grouping, data from the 24-week study 1218.46 were combined with data from its 52-week extension trial 1218.52, providing efficacy data for up to 78 weeks. During this period, the patients who received metformin monotherapy (1000 mg bid) in both studies showed a sustained reduction in Hba~1c~ and FPG over time, even though the changes from baseline were smaller than those in patients who received combined treatment with linagliptin and metformin in both trials. For a detailed description of the efficacy results in the combined studies 1218.46 and 1218.52 see below.

In addition, active-controlled 2-year efficacy data are available from study 1218.20.

*Change from baseline in Hba~1c~ and FPG over time in the combined studies 1218.18 and 1218.40 (EFF-C10)*

For the patients who had received linagliptin + metformin + SU (Lina+Met+SU) in study 1218.18, a decrease in mean Hba~1c~ of -0.72% from baseline to Week 24 was observed. When these patients continued with the same treatment in study 1218.40, Hba~1c~ levels remained reasonably stable, with a mean change from baseline in Hba~1c~ of -0.64% after 102 weeks. Hence the analysis demonstrated persistent glucose-lowering efficacy of the triple combination of Lina+Met+SU.

While the analyses of the changes from baseline in Hba~1c~ were in line with the long-term results seen for linagliptin mono therapy (1218.16+1218.40, Section 4.5.1) and the dual therapy of Lina+Met (EFF-C8), the long-term data for mean changes in FPG were different and seem to indicate dwindling efficacy. The mean changes in FPG were small, particularly in patients who had received the triple combination for 102 weeks. This result differs substantially from the FPG changes observed for the Lina+Met therapy (-15.9 mg/dL at 102 weeks). This difference might originate from the populations included into studies 1218.17 (Lina+Met) and 1218.18 (Lina+Met+SU). The proportions of patients who had been diagnosed with diabetes for more than 5 years were 54.9% (1218.17) and 73.3% (1218.18). Also, at the time of inclusion into the initial trial, 31.4% in the 1218.17 and 100% of patients in study 1218.18 took 2 or more OADs. Thus it appears that the diabetes was at a more advanced stage in patients included in the 1218.18 trial than in the 1218.17 trial. The smaller FPG change seen in study 1218.18 despite a similar Hba~1c~ reduction compared with the other 3 studies could indicate that in study 1218.18, linagliptin had a greater proportional effect on post-prandial glucose levels.

# 5. OVERVIEW OF SAFETY

Generally, the analysis of safety is presented separately for linagliptin monotherapy, metformin monotherapy, the combination of linagliptin+metformin, and the triple combination of linagliptin+metformin+SU. Since metformin has been in clinical use for some 50 years in the European Union, no dedicated studies to assess the safety of metformin were conducted. However, information on metformin monotherapy is included in some of the safety analysis sets (SAFs) for the linagliptin mono and combination arms.

The linagliptin monotherapy submission comprised in total 37 studies (24 phase I, 4 phase II, and 9 phase III trials) involving 6198 patients with T2DM of whom 4061 took Lina 5 mg. The analysis of safety of linagliptin monotherapy presented here is based on data that were already presented as part of the linagliptin monotherapy submission, namely a pooled analysis of all placebo-controlled studies with Lina 5 mg conducted in patients with T2DM (SAF-2). It included 12 trials, among them 7 phase III trials, 3 phase II trials, and 2 phase I trials with a total of 2566 patients treated with Lina 5 mg qd.

In addition, new long-term safety data for linagliptin monotherapy are derived from a pooled analysis of studies 1218.16 (placebo-controlled 24 weeks) and 1218.17 (placebo-controlled 24 weeks, linagliptin as add-on to metformin), together with their 78-week open-label extensions as study 1218.40 (SAF-M1). The analysis was confined to patients who had received Lina 5 mg (n=336) or Lina 5 mg+Met (n=523) already in the initial trials. Hence, the treatment groups analysed in SAF-M1 were neither the result of a randomisation procedure nor were they placebo-controlled. However, otherwise they were parallel groups that were subjected to the same protocol in the extension phase. For the linagliptin monotherapy submission the cut-off date was 12 Jan 2010, for this submission, the cut-off date (second interim) was 15 Oct 2010; the final trial report will be available shortly.

The evaluation of safety of the Lina+Met combination, 14 clinical studies were analysed and arranged into 10 study groupings (SAF-C1 to SAF-C9 and SEA-2) including pooled analyses of several trials. An overview of the safety groupings is given in Table 5: 1 below.

The focus of the presentation is on SAF-C1, the safety data of the pivotal trial 1218.17 (n=700) which compared linagliptin with placebo in patients who took metformin as background medication over 24 weeks. This safety analysis is supported by data from a pooled analysis (SAF-C5) of all placebo-controlled trials that used metformin either as background medication or as study treatment (n=1971). Additionally, for the establishment of the side effect profile of Lina 5 mg+Met, an analysis set (SEA-2, n=1905) was generated that was based on SAF-C5 but considered solely patients who took linagliptin 5 mg resulting in an exclusion of 66 patients from study 1218.6 who took linagliptin 10 mg.

Data on long-term use of the Lina+Met combination is available from study grouping SAF-C6 (n=433) which combined data of the 24 week study 1218.46 with its double-blind extension trial 1218.52 to provide data over 78 weeks. This is supplemented by safety data from study 1218.20 that compared Lina+Met with Glim+Met over 104 weeks. The full analysis and description of all safety groupings is available in the SCS [Module 2.7.4, Section 1.1.6].

Safety of the triple combination therapy linagliptin+metformin+SU is evaluated based on SAF-C7 that contained the 1218.18 trial, a 24-week placebo-controlled trial of linagliptin added to background treatment of metformin + SU.

The treated set (TS) was used for the analysis of safety and comprised all patients who received at least one dose of study medication. The screened set (SCR) consisted of all patients who were screened for the trial, had given informed consent, and had completed at least some screening procedures. Concomitant therapies were coded using the World Health Organization (WHO) Drug Dictionary, version Sept 2010. Concomitant diagnoses and adverse events were coded using the Medical Dictionary of Regulatory Affairs (MedDRA) version 13.1, with system organ class (SOC) and preferred term (PT).

For the analysis of adverse events, a treatment-emergent approach was chosen: All events with an onset date after the first dose of study medication up to a period of 7 days after the last dose of study medication were assigned to the randomised treatment period. Adverse events that occurred before first intake of randomised study medication were assigned to 'pretreatment'; and adverse events that occurred after the last intake of study medication +7 days were assigned to 'post-treatment'. For patients participating in an extension trial, adverse events with an onset on or after the first day of treatment in the extension trial were assigned to the treatment of the extension trial.

Some events were of particular importance to the safety of linagliptin either because of scientific considerations or because of concerns that had arisen with other DPP-4 inhibitors. These 'adverse events of special interest' were hypoglycaemia, hypersensitivity reactions, renal events (including laboratory evaluations), hepatic events (including laboratory evaluations), severe cutaneous adverse reactions, and pancreatitis. For 'adverse events of special interest' other than hypoglycaemia, the analysis was based on standardised MedDRA queries (narrow Standard MedDRA Queries [SMQ]). Details on the definitions are available in the SCS [Module 2.7.4, Section 1.1.7].

A particular focus was also on the identification of hypoglycaemic events. For all studies (except 1218.6 which is therefore excluded from the analysis of hypoglycemic events), in addition to the standard reporting of adverse events, the investigators were asked to assess whether an adverse event was related to or a result of a hypoglycaemic episode. A detailed description of events contributing to the category 'investigator-defined hypoglycaemia' is provided in the SCS [Module 2.7.4, Section 1.1.7]. For the active-controlled trial 1218.20 (SAF-C2), a confirmatory analysis for hypoglycaemic events was performed as planned after 52 weeks and 104 weeks.

Since metformin is known to cause gastrointestinal side effects, additional analyses were performed. Drug-related gastrointestinal adverse events were analysed based on the SSC 'gastrointestinal adverse reactions'which included the MedDRA PTs 'vomiting', 'nausea', diarrhoea', and 'abdominal pain'. Furthermore, for the identification of side effects, the listed side effects of metformin [R11-0761, Module 2.7.5] were compared with the adverse events profile observed in SEA-2.

Note that the individual study results were re-calculated for this evaluation of safety, using the project database which takes into account information that was not availabe at the time of study database lock, rather than the individual study databases. Moreover, the analyses presented are partly based on pooled study data, modified statistical models, different analysis sets, and/or different imputation methods compared with the study reports. Therefore, small numerical differences between the study results presented in the individual study reports and here may occur.


Analysis of cardiovascular risk

The evaluation of cardiovascular risk potentially associated with the use of linagliptin received particular attention. For the phase III studies, all reported treatment-emergent fatal events and events suggestive of stroke or myocardial ischaemia, including myocardial infarction, were reviewed and adjudicated in a blinded fashion by an independent Clinical Event Committee (CEC) which comprised 3 independent experts in cardiology (CEC Cardiology) and 3 independent experts of neurology (CEC Neurology). The CEC Cardiology reviewed all fatal events (without stroke events) and events suspected of myocardial ischaemia; the CEC Neurology adjudicated events suggestive of stroke (fatal and not-fatal strokes). The CEC evaluated whether pre-specified definitions for cardiovascular death, stroke, or myocardial ischaemia were fulfilled. Further details on the CEC, responsibilities, and clinical event definitions, are provided in the CEC Charter, which is included in the appendix of the CV meta-analysis report. Based on the blinded adjudications of the CEC a meta-analysis was conducted. The primary endpoint was a composite endpoint consisting of cardiovascular death (fatal stroke and fatal MI), non-fatal MI, non-fatal stroke, and unstable angina. In addition, the FDA-defined custom endpoint of major cardiovascular events (MACE) was analysed. Details regarding the statistical analyses and the full set of results are available in the CV meta-analysis report [U10-1736, Module 5.3.5.3]; however a summary of these results is provided in Section 5.6.

Laboratory evaluation

Samples for the determination of safety laboratory parameters (haematology, clinical chemistry, and urinalysis) were taken at almost all study visits in all trials. Laboratory values taken after the first dose of randomised treatment up to a period of 7 days after the last intake of treatment were assigned to the treatment phase. Laboratory analyses were performed on all safety groupings (SAF-C1 to SAF-C9, and SEA-2), however to a different extent per grouping: descriptive statistics and transitions relative to the reference range were performed for SAF-C2, SAF-C5, SAF-C6, and SAF-C7. Possible Clinically Significant Abnormalities (PCSA) and changes in renal function were performed on all SAFs except SAF-C9. Analyses to detect potential liver toxicity (including potential Hy's law cases) were performed on all SAFs. If applicable, reference to respective adverse event analyses were made for discussion of observations of laboratory analyses. Further details are available in the SCS [Module 2.7.4, Section 3].

Vital signs and physical examination

Evaluations of vital signs and physical examination were performed for SAF-C1 to SAF-C7. Changes from baseline in systolic and diastolic blood pressure and pulse rate were analysed descriptively. Clinically relevant abnormal findings in 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) measurements and in the physical examination were to be reported as adverse events. Further details are available in the SCS [Module 2.7.4, Section 4].

### 5.1.1 Exposure to linagliptin monotherapy

The planned study durations in the set of placebo-controlled trials (SAF-2) ranged from 12 days (1218.2) to 24 weeks (4 pivotal trials with different antidiabetic background medications). Trial 1218.23 had a planned duration of 52 weeks, however for SAF-2 only 12 week data of the placebo-controlled period were considered. For SAF-2 overall, 88.8% (placebo) and 92.9% (linagliptin) of patients completed the studies; 11.2% (placebo) and 7.1% (linagliptin) discontinued treatment prematurely. The proportions of patients who discontinued because of adverse events were slightly higher in the placebo group than in the linagliptin group (2.6% vs. 2.2%).

## 5.2 SAFETY OF METFORMIN MONOTHERAPY

As metformin is in clinical use for some 50 years in the EU, its safety profile is well established. Clinical trials have shown that metformin is generally well-tolerated and safe.  The majority of side effects are gastrointestinal in nature and include nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, appetite loss, and taste disturbance. A large number of these gastrointestinal effects are mild and can be reduced by taking metformin with food, initiating therapy with the lowest possible dose, and slow dose titration. Nevertheless, approximately 5% of patients discontinue metformin therapy because they are intolerant to its gastrointestinal side effects [P97-1130, P10-09483, R10-4882, Module 2.7.5]. Furthermore, due to the gastrointestinal side effects of metformin, many patients are unable to take the recommended daily dose of at least 1500 mg metformin [R10-4375, Module 2.7.5].

The most serious adverse event associated with metformin use is lactic acidosis. Lactic acidosis is rare with approximately 0.03 incidences per 1000 patient-years, but is associated with a high mortality. The risk of lactic acidosis is a concern in cases of metformin overdose or in special populations, such as patients with moderate renal impairment and those with congestive heart failure. Thus, while it is a serious adverse reaction, the risk of lactic acidosis can be minimised by adherence to prescribing recommendations and close monitoring of renal and cardiac function [R09-4255, R10-0861, R10-4375, Module 2.7.5].

In several studies, patients taking metformin experienced significantly fewer diabetes-related complications, including hypoglycaemia, than those taking other oral antidiabetic medications [R10-4882, Module 2.7.5]. In the United Kingdom Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS), metformin treatment was associated with 29.8 diabetes-related complications per 1000 patient-years, combined SU and insulin monotherapy was associated with 40.1 events per 1000 patient-years, and diet alone was associated with 43.3 events per 1000 patient years. Furthermore, UKPDS provided evidence that patients treated with metformin experience a significant reduction in the absolute risk of myocardial infarction [R09-4255, R10-0861, Module 2.7.5].

For this submission no dedicated studies with metformin monotherapy were conducted, however information on the safety of metformin monotherapy is included in the safety groupings in the respective treatment arms of some studies.

#### 5.3.2.2 Drug-related adverse events

The incidences of adverse events that were assessed as related to study drug by the investigators was comparable between the Met (8.7%) and the Lina+Met (7.3%) group. The most frequent drug-related adverse events in the metformin group were metabolism and nutrition disorders (Met 10.30 vs. Lina+Met 5.37) and within this SOC hyperglycaemia (5.98 vs. 1.97) and hypoglycaemia (2.96 vs. 2.37) were the most frequent events. In the Lina+Met group the most frequent drug-related adverse events were gastrointestinal disorders (Met 6.48 vs. Lina+Met 7.99). Within this SOC diarrhoea was the most frequent event with similar rates per 100 PY in both treatment groups (2.98 vs. 2.37). Overall incidences above 1% were observed for nervous system disorders (2.12 vs. 2.97) with dizziness (0.42 vs. 1.97) being more frequent in the Lina+Met group. The patterns seen in the pooled set of metformin background trials (SAF-C5) are qualitatively and quantitatively similar to those observed in the pivotal trial 1218.17 (SAF-C1).

#### 5.3.2.3 Adverse events leading to discontinuation

The percentage of patients who discontinued because of adverse events was low in both treatment groups (Met 2.6% vs. Lina+Met 2.4%). Gastrointestinal disorders were the most frequent adverse events that led to discontinuation, however with similar rates in both treatment groups (rate/100PY Met 2.11 vs. Lina+Met 1.96). ### 5.3.3 Clinical laboratory evaluation and vital signs

Clinical laboratory evaluation included analysis of haematology, clinical chemistry, and urinalysis. #### 5.3.3.1 Clinical laboratory evaluation and vital signs for linagliptin monotherapy

For the linagliptin monotherapy submission, analyses of laboratory data were performed on a number of safety groupings; for SAF-2 (all placebo-controlled trials, placebo vs. linagliptin), transitions relative to the reference range, possible clinically significant abnormalities (PCSAs), DILIS, and changes in renal function were analysed. In addition, adverse events leading to discontinuation were checked manually for laboratory abnormalities.

With regard to transitions, overall no clinically relevant findings or marked differences between placebo and linagliptin were observed for any of the measured parameters (haematology, electrolytes, enzymes, substrates, and urine analysis). Further details are provided in [U10-1607, Module 5.3.5.3, Table 6.2.2.1].

PCSAs were reported for a several parameters (haematology and clinical chemistry), with very low (<3.0% of patients) and comparable frequencies in both treatment groups. A possibly clinically significant increase in triglyceride levels was reported for 8.8% (PBO) and 7.8% (Lina). There were no patients with PCSAs reported in urine analysis. None of the observed increases or decreases was considered to be of clinical relevance. Further details are provided in [U10-1607, Module 5.3.5.3, Table 6.1.2.1].

No patient fulfilled the criteria for Hy's law; the vast majority of patients (about 99%) in both treatment groups were categorised as having enzyme and total bilirubin levels of grade 0, indicating a very low risk of a potential liver injury. In both treatment groups, some patients had liver enzyme values of grade 1 (placebo ≤1.0 %; linagliptin ≤0.8%). A few patients (<5) in both treatment groups had elevated liver enzyme levels but no pattern was discernable (details are available in [U10-1607, Module 5.3.5.3, Table 6.3.2.1]). There was no evidence for a treatment-induced difference in renal function; details are given in [U10-1607, Module 5.3.5.3, Table 6.6.2.1].

For SAF-M1, the clinical laboratory evaluation focussed on parameters of liver function and of renal function. Liver enzyme levels were assessed in regard to transitions in relation to reference ranges, changes in mean values over time and from baseline, and the identification of DILIs. Overall the groups were comparable; differences between the Lina and Lina+Met groups exceeding 2% in the analysis of transistions from baseline to last value on treatment were seen for AST shifts from low or normal to high (Lina 2.9%, Lina+Met 5.3%) and for total bilirubin shifts from high and normal to low (Lina 3.7%, Lina+Met 8.5%). The treatment groups were very similar in regard to mean changes from baseline of liver enzymes. The evaluation for cases of DILI showed similar results in both groups; no patient qualified as Hy’s law case, however 1 patient in the Lina group (PatID 62965) had ALT or AST increases above 3 ULN and concurrent elevation of bilirubin of more than 2 ULN. The frequency of patients with ALT or AST above 3 ULN was identical (1.5%) in both treament groups. [U11-1152, Module 5.3.5.3, Tables 8.3.1.1, 8.3.1.2, 8.3.1.3, 8.3.1.4]

The treatment groups were very similar in renal function parameters. Of the patients with normal renal function at baseline, 18.7% (Lina) and 23.2% (Lina+Met) had a shift to mild renal impairment at their last value on treatment and no patient (Lina) and 4 patients (1.3%) in the Lina+Met group had a shift to moderate renal impairment. ## 5.5 ADVERSE EVENTS OF SPECIAL INTEREST

The analysis of the adverse events of special interest was based on narrow SMQs and not on investigator-reported significant adverse events to cover the medical concepts for hypersensitivity reactions, renal events, hepatic events, severe cutaneous adverse reactions, and pancreatitis in a more complete way. *Hypoglycaemic events*

The analysis of hypoglycaemic events was based on investigator-reporting. ## 5.6 RESULTS OF CARDIOVASCULAR SAFETY ANALYSIS

Eight trials with a total of 5239 patients with T2DM were included in a cardiovascular meta-analysis. The primary endpoint was based on adjudicated events and was a composite endpoint consisting of cardiovascular death (including fatal stroke and fatal MI), non-fatal MI, non-fatal stroke, and hospitalisation due to unstable angina. This analysis indicated that treatment with linagliptin was not associated with an increased cardiovascular risk compared with a pooled comparator group (placebo, glimepiride, and voglibose). For further details, including results on subgroup analyses, refer to the report [U10-1736, Module 5.3.5.3].

## 5.8 RESULTS OF THE ANALYSIS OF SIDE EFFECTS

The analysis of side effects was based on SEA-2 which comprised all trials comparing placebo+metformin with linagliptin 5 mg+metformin. SEA-2 is very similar to SAF-C5 but excluded all patients that had taken linagliptin doses other than linagliptin 5 mg. Based on this safety set, a special analysis was undertaken to identify any adverse events that are possibly associated with linagliptin+metformin treatment. The analysis compared results for 1322 patients treated with linagliptin 5 mg+metformin and 583 patients treated with placebo+metformin. Since SEA-2 set does not provide a true placebo versus Lina+Met comparison, potential side effects were identified (according to Criteria 1 to 3, see below) by comparing frequencies of adverse events with linagliptin+metformin treatment to the frequencies of adverse events with placebo+metformin treatment, for adverse events which are not known to be related to metformin treatment (for these, placebo+metformin is set as “placebo“).The criteria employed were:

(1) Adverse events that occurred with a frequency of 2% or more with linagliptin+metformin treatment and had a 2-fold higher frequency in the linagliptin+metformin group than in the comparator group, or when the frequency in the comparator group was zero, and/or

(2) Adverse events that were likely related based on medical plausibility, and/or

(3) Adverse events that had a consistent pattern across antidiabetic background treatments; i.e., their frequencies in the linagliptin groups were consistently higher than in the placebo groups with every antidiabetic background medication

## 5.9 PREGNANCY AND LACTATION

There are no adequate and well-controlled studies with linagliptin in pregnant women. Because animal reproduction studies are not always predictive of human response, linagliptin like other antidiabetic medications should be avoided during pregnancy.

Pregnant and nursing mothers were excluded from the studies in the linagliptin clinical development programme both for the monotherapy as well as for the combination therapy with metformin. In all studies, women of childbearing potential participating in the trial were asked to use appropriate contraceptive precautions to prevent pregnancies. All women with child-bearing potential had to undergo pregnancy testing at screening, and all were tested negative. However, in total 9 women became pregnant while exposed to study medication (6 pregnancies were reported in the studies analysed for the linagliptin + metformin combination therapy, in addition 3 more pregnancies were reported in studies not analysed for this FDC dossier; data status 02 March 2011). Of these pregnancies one child is still to be born, 4 children are healthy (n=3) or the health status is unknown (n=1); the pregnancies were aborted (n=2), the child was born dead (n=1), or the child had congenital abnormalities (n=1). Full details including narratives are available in the SCS [Module 2.7.4, Section 5.4.1].

There are limited data from the use of metformin in pregnant women. Metformin was not teratogenic in rats at a dose of 200 mg/kg/day associated with 4 times human exposure. At higher doses (500 and 1000 mg/kg/day, associated with 11 and 23 times human exposure), teratogenicity of metformin was observed in the rat [U10-2386].

No studies on lactating animals have been performed with the combination of metformin and linagliptin. Non-clinical studies with the individual active substances have shown excretion of both metformin and linagliptin into milk in lactating rats [U08-1929; R10-5537, Module 2.7.5]. Metformin is excreted with milk in humans [R10-4318, Module 2.7.5]. It is not known whether linagliptin is excreted into human milk. Linagliptin should not be used during breast feeding.

5.10 OVERDOSAGE, DRUG ABUSE AND DEPENDENCE, WITHDRAWAL

*Overdose*

Based on its mode of action and on its pharmacokinetic characteristics, it is unlikely that acute overdosing of linagliptin will affect vital functions or cause life threatening hypoglycaemia. In the event of an overdose, the usual supportive measures should be employed, e.g. removal of unabsorbed material from the gastrointestinal tract, clinical monitoring, and institution of supportive therapy if required.

In a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in healthy male subjects, single doses of linagliptin of up to 600 mg (equivalent to 120 tablets) were administered orally and were overall well tolerated. Generally, the occurrence of adverse events was not dose dependent. Neither clinically relevant changes of laboratory parameters nor of ECG parameters were observed (BI trial 1218.1) [U05-2072, Module 5.3.3.1].

For metformin, information on overdose is given in [R10-5537, Module 2.7.5]. Briefly, hypoglycaemia has not been seen with doses up to 85 g. However, metformin overdose may lead to lactic acidosis which requires immediate hospitalisation. The most effective method to remove lactate and metformin is haemodialysis.

*Drug abuse*

Since the pharmacological properties, as well as non-clinical and clinical data do not indicate an impact on the central nervous system suggestive for stimulant, depressant, hallucinogenic or mood-elevating effects the potential for abuse is believed to be minimal. For metformin, information is available in [R10-5537, Module 2.7.5].

Neither linagliptin nor metformin is a controlled substance. Linagliptin has not been systematically studied in animals or humans for its potential for abuse, tolerance, or physical dependence. In clinical trials to date no potential for abuse was observed with the linagliptin am metformin combination.

*Withdrawal*

In the trials performed with the combination of linagliptin and metformin no withdrawal phenomena were observed. Information on metformin is given in [R10-5537, Module 2.7.5].

5.11 EFFECTS ON ABILITY TO DRIVE OR OPERATE MACHINERY OR IMPAIRMENT OF MENTAL ABILITY

No studies on the effects on the ability to drive and use machines have been performed with linagliptin. Information on metformin is given in [R10-5537, Module 2.7.5].

5.12 POSTMARKETING EXPERIENCE

The application for a marketing authorisation for linagliptin monotherapy is currently under evaluation and hence no post-marketing data are available. As this is a new drug application for the linagliptin and metformin fixed-dose combination, no post-marketing data are available. Conversely, metformin has been used in Europe for over 50 years and extensive post-marketing data is available for. Refer to the current SPC for further information [R10-5537, Module 2.7.5].

6. BENEFITS AND RISKS CONCLUSIONS

The clinical development programme for the combination therapy comprised 14 clinical trials, among them, 2 phase II-trials, 4 phase III-trials and 2 long-term follow-up trials of the phase III-studies. In total 3529 patients with type 2 diabetes received treatment with linagliptin and metformin (Lina+Met). Overall, 2694 patients were treated for at least 24 weeks, 2081 patients for at least 52 weeks, and 1756 patients for more than 78 weeks. As evidenced by the demographic and baseline characteristics, the clinical programme included a representative spectrum of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus.

*Efficacy*

Overall, 7 trials contributed to the demonstration of efficacy of the combination therapy with Lina+Met. The pivotal placebo-controlled trial 1218.17 investigated the efficacy of linagliptin as add-on therapy to metformin over 24 weeks. Additional key trials for the establishment of efficacy were studies 1218.62, 1218.46, and 1218.20. Trial 1218.62, a placebo-controlled study over 12 weeks, showed comparable efficacy of the linagliptin 2.5 mg twice daily (bid) regimen and the linagliptin 5 mg qd regimen when added to metformin. Study 1218.46 tested 2 Lina+Met combinations versus Met in a partial first-line setting. Trial 1218.20 was a double-blind active-controlled trial that tested Lina+Met against glimepiride+metformin (Glim+Met) over 104 weeks of treatment.

Long-term efficacy of the Lina+Met combination is shown by data from the patients who completed study 1218.17 and continued into the open-label 78-week extension study 1218.40. Furthermore longterm data (54 weeks) are available from study 1218.52, which is a double-blind metformin monotherapy-controlled extension trial in patients who completed the 24-week treatment period of study 1218.46 without requiring rescue medication.

Efficacy of Lina+Met in combination with SU over 24 weeks was investigated in study 1218.18, where linagliptin 5 mg qd or placebo were added to ongoing therapy with metformin and an SU. Data on long-term efficacy is available from patients who completed study 1218.18 and continued into trial 1218.40.

*Changes in Hba~1c~*

The pivotal trial 1218.17 showed that linagliptin added to ongoing metformin background therapy was superior to metformin alone in regard to the Hba~1c~ change from baseline. After 24 weeks of treatment a statistically significant and clinically meaningful adjusted mean treatment difference in Hba~1c~ of -0.64% in favour of Lina+Met combination was observed. Study 1218.62 demonstrated equivalence of Lina 2.5 mg bid to Lina 5 mg qd. The adjusted mean difference in Hba~1c~ changes from baseline to Week 12 was -0.74% (Lina 2.5 bid+Met vs. Met). Study 1218.46 was conducted in a partial first-line setting (47.5% treatment-naïve patients) and yielded similar results to the pivotal trial although the treatment effect in the Lina+Met combination therapy groups versus metformin was slightly smaller than in the pivotal trial with -0.58% (L2.5+M500 vs. M500) and -0.51% (L2.5+M1000 vs. M1000), p<0.001 for both comparisons. The Lina+Met combination therapy groups also achieved a substantially greater reduction in Hba~1c~ than linagliptin 5 mg monotherapy with differences of -0.77% (L2.5+M500 vs. Lina 5) and -1.14% (L2.5+M1000 vs. Lina 5); p-value was below 0.0001 for both comparisons.

Study 1218.20 demonstrated non-inferiority in the reduction of Hba~1c~ of Lina+Met to Glim+Met. After 104 weeks of treatment, a mean Hba~1c~ decrease from baseline was observed in both treatment groups (Lina+Met -0.16% vs. Glim+Met -0.36%; adjusted mean difference 0.20%). The 2-sided 97.5% CI (0.09%, 0.30%) for the treatment difference excluded the pre-specified non-inferiority margin of 0.35% and the p-value for the non-inferiority test (p = 0.0004) was below α=0.0125. It was therefore concluded that Lina+Met was non-inferior to Glim+Met.

Across all studies, including the pivotal trial 1218.17, the additional reductions in Hba~1c~ achieved with Lina+Met combination therapies over Met monotherapy (as background or as study treatment) were in the range of 0.51% to 0.64%. Hence all trials demonstrated a clinically meaningful reduction in Hba~1c~ with combination therapy of linagliptin and metformin.

*FPG and other measures of efficacy*

Also for FPG substantial and clinically meaningful reductions were observed in all trials. The reductions in FPG were in the range of -13.7 mg/dL after 12 weeks to -21.1 mg/dL after 24 weeks of treatment; the p-values for the comparisons Of Lina+Met versus Met were always below 0.005. Hence the results seen with FPG support the findings of the primary endpoint analysis for Hba~1c~.

Also the analyses of other measures of efficacy such as percentage of patients achieving glycaemic control defined as Hba~1c~ below 7% or below 6.5% and post-prandial glucose (2hPPG) measurements corroborated the superior efficacy of Lina+Met over Met. In addition, substantially fewer patients in the Lina+Met groups than in the Met groups needed rescue medication. For study 1218.17, only 7.8% of patients in the Lina+Met group but 18.9% of patients in the Met group used rescue medication. This was confirmed by results from the other trials.

In addition, the combination therapy of Lina+Met proved to be weight neutral in comparison with metformin (1218.17: -0.4 kg in both treatment groups). Weight neutrality could be confirmed over 104 weeks of treatment (1218.20) in which patients receiving Lina+Met lost on average 1.4 kg while patients taking Glim+Met gained 1.3 kg, resulting in a treatment difference of -2.7 kg.

Superior efficacy of Lina+Met over Met could also be shown in relevant subgroups of patients. Thus factors such as age, gender, race (White vs. Asian), ethnicity, geographical region, and baseline BMI did not have an influence on the treatment effect of the Lina+Met combination therapy. Furthermore, the presence of washout of prior OADs and the time since diagnosis of diabetes did not influence the treatment effect of Lina+Met combination therapy.

The reductions of Hba~1c~ were largely independent of metformin dose (500 mg, 850 mg or 1000 mg). The magnitude of the Lina+Met treatment effect was largely consistent across the different metformin doses with adjusted mean changes from baseline of -0.51% to -0.70% after 24 weeks of treatment. A post-hoc analysis of study 1218.46 showed that the combination of linagliptin 2.5 mg bid + metformin 500 mg bid was at least as effective in reducing Hba~1c~ and FPG levels as monotherapy with metformin 1000 mg bid. Therefore, linagliptin 2.5 mg/metformin 500 mg FDC could be considered a useful alternative to metformin monotherapy for patients intolerant to the recommended daily metformin dose. In sumamry, the data support all 3 intended FDC posologies.

Analysis of the long-term trials demonstrated that the Lina+Met treatment effect in regard to the reduction of Hba~1c~ and FPG persists over at least 104 weeks. This could also be demonstrated across trials for other efficacy outcomes such as percentage of patients achieving glycaemic control, need for rescue medication, and for weight neutrality.

Trial 1218.18 demonstrated the superior efficacy of a triple combination of Lina+Met+SU over Met+SU with an adjusted mean difference in Hba~1c~ of -0.62, p<0.0001. The superiority of the triple combination was also seen for all other measures of efficacy, i.e. FPG, percentage of patients achieving glycaemic control, use of rescue therapy, and body weight. The persistence of the treatment effects could be demonstrated for up to 102 weeks of treatmentin trial 1218.40.

**Safety**

The evaluation of safety of Lina+Met combination therapy for the treatment of patients with T2DM was based on 6 phase I studies, 2 phase II studies, and 6 phase III studies. Data were analysed in 10 study groupings: SAF-C1 to SAF-C9, including the analysis of safety for the triple combination of linagliptin+metformin+SU (SAF-C7). A subset of SAF-C5 (only including patients treated with the linagliptin 5 mg daily as this is the proposed dose) was used for identification of potential side effects (SEA-2). Results of the linagliptin mono SCS have been summarised based on SAF-2. In addition, new long-term safety data of linagliptin monotherapy over 78 weeks of treatment was analysed (SAF-M1).

*Linagliptin monotherapy*

In SAF-M1, infections and infestations, musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders, gastrointestinal disorders, and nervous system disorders occurred with a higher frequency in the Lina+Met group than in the Lina group. However, these apparent differences in adverse events might be due to differences for concomitant diagnoses at baseline: infections and infestations (Lina 7.4% vs. Lina+Met 11.1%), musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders (17.0% vs. 21.2%), gastrointestinal disorders (13.4% vs. 19.5%), and nervous system disorders (7.1% vs. 12.4%) (Data from study reports 1218.16 [U10-1103; Module 5.3.5.1] and 1218.17 [U09-2533, Module 5.3.5.1]) were less frequent in the Lina group than in the Lina+Met group. In addition, respiratory, thoracic, and mediastinal disorders (6.8% vs. 10.9%) were more frequent in the Lina+Met group due to a higher frequency of cough (2.7% vs. 6.1%) in the Lina+Met group. Hyperglycaemia was the most frequently reported preferred term in both groups (Lina: 36.6%, Lina+Met 29.3%).

Taken together, the analyis of SAF-M1 confirmed the safety analysis for the linagliptin monotherapy. However, on the level of preferred terms, higher incidences of nasopharyngitis, urinary tract infection, influenza, diarrhea, and cough were observed with Lina+Met combination therapy. Analysis of adverse events by time interval did neither show clinically meaningful increases over time nor notable differences between treatments.

*Linagliptin + metformin combination therapy*

Analysis of safety was initially based on the pivotal trial (1218.17, SAF-C1) and this analysis was compared with data obtained from SAF-C5 (and SEA-2), a pooled analysis of all metformin-controlled trials.

In SAF-C1, the most frequent adverse events were infections and infestations (22.0% vs. 21.6%). Gastrointestinal disorders also occurred with almost identical incidences in both treatment groups (11.9% vs. 11.1%). Differences of 2% or more were observed for musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders which were more frequent in the Lina+Met group (7.9% vs. 11.1%). Also injury, poisoning and procedural complications (2.3% vs. 5.0%) and respiratory, thoracic and mediastinal disorders (2.8% vs. 4.8%) were slightly more frequent in the Lina+Met group. Conversely, metabolism and nutrition disorders were substantially less frequent in the Lina+Met group than in the Met group (23.7% vs. 10.7%) mainly due to a lower incidence of hyperglycaemia (16.4% vs. 6.1%). Also hypoglycaemia was less frequently with Lina+Met combination therapy than with metformin alone (2.8% vs. 0.6%). Furthermore, investigations (8.5% vs. 4.0%) and psychiatric disorders (2.8% vs. 0.8%) were less frequent with the combination therapy than in the Met group. Comparing the frequencies of adverse events in the Lina+Met group of the pivotal trial with those of the linagliptin group in SAF-2 demonstrates very similar or even lower incidences across system organ classes.

The comparison of the adverse event incidences in the pivotal trial (SAF-C1) with the incidences in the pooled analysis of all placebo-controlled trials with metformin background (SAF-C5, SEA-2) indicated a consistent safety profile of the Lina+Met combination. Overall incidences of adverse events and SOCs were generally very similar in SAF-C1, SAF-C5, and SEA-2. Differences in adverse events incidences found in the pivotal trial were also seen in the other analysis sets. These observations were confirmed in the analysis of the other safety sets. The analysis of the safety by relevant subgroups indicated that the safety profile of the Lina+Met combination was not influenced by age, gender, race (White and Asian), ethnicity, geographic region, renal impairment, or metformin dose.

In total, 10 cases of pancreas disorders were reported: 8 cases occurring during treatment with Lina+Met, and 2 cases during treatment with Glim+Met. In the post-treatment period, 2 cases of pancreas disorders were reported. None of the cases has been necrotising, haemorrhagic, or fatal. Photosensitivity reactions were reported by 4 patients: 3 cases occurring during treatment with Lina+Met and 1 case during treatment with Met. None of the events was serious, 1 event was considered drug-related. Angioedema was reported by 2 patients during treatment with linagliptin + metformin; one event was serious, both events were not assessed as drug-related.

In total, 18 deaths were reported: 14 deaths during treatment and 4 deaths occurring in the post-treatment period. In addition, 2 more deaths occurred during washout. Of the 14 deaths occurring during treatment, 9 deaths were reported during treatment with Lina+Met and 5 deaths during treatment with Met (monotherapy or in combination with glimepiride); however exposure-corrected incidences of death were very similar across treatment groups within studies. No death was considered related to the study medication.

Taken together, in the clinical studies included in this submission, the overall incidences of adverse events, drug-related adverse events, adverse events of severe intensity, adverse events leading to discontinuation, and serious adverse events were largely comparable for combination therapy with linagliptin+metformin versus monotherapies. The data reveal no indication of an increased safety burden for the combination over that seen with the components.

The observed adverse events profile is in accordance with the known profile of individual components such as gastrointestinal disorders (e.g., decreased appetite, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea) and pruritus associated with metformin; and nasopharyngitis, hypersensitivity, cough, and pancreatitis for linagliptin. Hypoglycemia is identified as a side effect only when linagliptin and metformin are combined with sulfonylureas.

In summary, the benefits of the combination therapy with linagliptin and metformin are greater reductions in Hba~1c~ and FPG over metformin and linagliptin monotherapies, low need for rescue therapy, weight neutrality, lower incidence of hypoglycemic events (vs glimepiride +metformin), good tolerability also among special populations, and the convenience and enhanced compliance expected with a single-tablet formulation.
